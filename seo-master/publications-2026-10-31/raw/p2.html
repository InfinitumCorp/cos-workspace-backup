In-Q-Tel Explained: The CIA's Venture Capital Arm

By William Aaron Turner, President of Infinitum Corporation October 25, 2026
Contents

    The Ukraine Files · Part 4 of 8

    By William Aaron Turner, President of Infinitum Corporation · The Ukraine Files: Full Series

    In 1999 the CIA created a nonprofit venture arm named after James Bond's gadget-maker. My X Article WTF is IN-Q-TEL follows that organization, In-Q-Tel (IQT), from its Virginia headquarters to the AI, drone and satellite companies it has backed, and from there to the battlefields of Ukraine. This post is the structured version: who In-Q-Tel is, what it funds, which portfolio companies show up in Ukraine, and what I argue it all adds up to.

    Key takeaways

    • In-Q-Tel is a not-for-profit corporation headquartered in the Tysons Corner area of Virginia, established by the CIA in 1999. For fiscal year 2025 it reported revenue of about $132.1 million, 86 percent from government grants and contributions.
    • Cumulative investments since 1999 exceed 800. Typical instruments are technology-development agreements of roughly $500,000 to $3 million, combined with equity or warrants.
    • In 2005 In-Q-Tel invested in Palantir and introduced it to the intelligence community. The article also names Anduril, Capella Space and ICEYE as In-Q-Tel-linked companies whose technology reaches Ukraine.
    • The article notes there is no open-source evidence that In-Q-Tel's subsidiaries operate inside Ukraine. My argument is about funding and technology, not boots on the ground.
    • The most contested part of the article, the "bio-nexus" claims, rests on Russian Ministry of Defense allegations that the article itself says have not been independently verified.

    What In-Q-Tel is

    The name is an intentional reference to "Q" in the James Bond films. The parent entity is In-Q-Tel, Inc., with subsidiaries that include IQTI LLC (an investment-holding vehicle), IQT LABS LLC (applied research), LAB41 LLC, SpaceNet LLC, and international units IQT International UK Ltd and IQT International Australia Ltd. The Board of Trustees has included former CIA Director George Tenet, former Joint Chiefs Chairman Michael Mullen and Michael Crow of Arizona State University. A small CIA liaison unit, the In-Q-Tel Interface Center, operates inside CIA headquarters and, as the article describes it, translates intelligence-community requirements into commercial problem sets.

    Senior executives named in the article include CEO Steve Bowsher, COO Matthew Strottman and Chief Investment Officer Dan Gwak. The portfolio leans toward enterprise software, AI and data analytics, then cybersecurity, semiconductors, space and remote sensing, energy, health and biotech, and robotics and autonomy.

    Ukraine as a testing ground

    The article quotes a defense analyst calling Ukraine "the world's most advanced live testing ground for AI-assisted warfare." The figures it cites:

    • Ukraine's forces receive roughly 12 to 15 terabytes of raw drone video daily, and platforms like OCHI have aggregated about two million hours of footage from over 15,000 drone crews.
    • More than 200 companies are developing AI-integrated drones in Ukraine, with over 70 systems deployed at the front.
    • In early 2026 Ukraine and Palantir established the Brave1 Dataroom, where developers train AI models on real combat data.
    • Ukraine's Delta battlefield management system has more than 200,000 users and receives up to 10,000 drone video feeds a day. German Army Deputy Commander Heico Hübner says detection-to-engagement time has fallen from hours to minutes or seconds.
    • Ukraine's military now launches up to 9,000 drones a day, according to the article, many operating autonomously.

    The "Terminator mode" test

    Per New Scientist reporting cited in the article, Ukrainian manufacturer Aero Center, led by CEO Alexander Kokhanovskyy, ran a one-time test near Bakhmut and Chasiv Yar of ten fully autonomous quadcopters with no communication link. Human-operated drones afterward confirmed "a couple of soldiers, one truck." Kokhanovskyy said it was a one-time experiment. Ukraine's Defence Ministry AI chief Danylo Tsvok has indicated Ukraine is considering removing the human-verification requirement.

    The portfolio companies in the story

    Palantir

    Founded in 2003 by Peter Thiel and Alex Karp, Palantir grew after In-Q-Tel's 2005 investment, with the CIA as its first major customer. The article says Palantir came to Ukraine in 2022, and that in May 2026 Zelensky met Karp in Kyiv alongside Mykhailo Fedorov to discuss military AI, air attack analysis and Brave1 Dataroom. More than a thousand Ukrainian personnel across brigade headquarters have used the software, and the article quotes Karp saying MetaConstellation was responsible for "most of the targeting" in Ukraine. Per documents from late 2022, Palantir and subsidiaries received more than $500 million for their initial months of work there. I argue this makes American technology and personnel directly involved in targeting decisions.

    Anduril

    Founded by Palmer Luckey, Anduril makes the Lattice software platform, the Ghost drone, the Bolt-M loitering munition and the Altius series. The article says In-Q-Tel is a documented investor. In 2025 the British government signed contracts of nearly £30 million with Anduril UK for Altius 600m and 700m munitions for donation to Ukraine, and Anduril's Rocket Motor Systems became a new supplier of solid-fuel motors for GLSDB bombs destined for Ukraine. Defense analysts cited believe that an unnamed customer of over 300 Bolt-M drones was Ukraine.

    Capella Space and ICEYE

    Both provide synthetic aperture radar imagery that sees through clouds and darkness. Capella partnered with In-Q-Tel in March 2021, and its satellites have been tasked many thousands of times over the Ukrainian battlefield. ICEYE, a Finnish company, operated 54 satellites as of August 2025, partnered with In-Q-Tel in 2021, supplied imagery before the February 2022 invasion, and has a memorandum with Ukraine's Ministry of Defence. A crowdfunding campaign led by the Serhiy Prytula foundation bought a satellite from ICEYE in 2022.

    How the structure keeps distance

    I argue that In-Q-Tel's design gives Washington plausible deniability:

    • Nonprofit framing: described as independent, yet created by the CIA and described in its own history as central to the Agency's technology edge.
    • Problem lists: the Interface Center hands requirements to In-Q-Tel, which finds startups to meet them.
    • Financial cushion: IRS filings show roughly $50 million to $64 million per year in funding, and In-Q-Tel claims to have leveraged more than $3.9 billion in private funds.
    • Contracting maze: portfolio companies such as Capella and ICEYE contract directly with agencies like the National Reconnaissance Office, so the CIA is not a named party.

    The contested claims

    The article's Part VII reports that in December 2022 the Russian Ministry of Defense listed In-Q-Tel Executive Vice President Tara O'Toole among participants in Ukraine's military biological programs. I flag the sourcing directly: the source is the Russian Ministry of Defense, the allegation "has not been independently verified," and the article states that what O'Toole and the others actually did in Ukraine is not public. Also in the article: Michael Crow was personally sanctioned by Russia in 2023 in his role as chairman of the In-Q-Tel board, after planning an Arizona State University extension in Kyiv that was halted by the invasion.

    Money and context

    The article places these companies inside a larger support structure: about $50.8 billion in U.S. military support as of March 2026, a NATO pledge of €140 billion for 2026-2027, and a €90 billion EU Ukraine Support Loan. It notes a gap between €400 billion promised and €180 billion delivered. In-Q-Tel-linked firms sit in that architecture, in the article's words, "as technology providers, not as primary funders or operational commanders."

    My conclusion

    My reading is that In-Q-Tel is a legal, public and quiet engine of the technology pipeline into Ukraine: the CIA's venture arm seeds technologies, startups build products, and Ukraine's military uses them. I write that the human cost, including false positives in AI targeting, is real, and that defense-tech companies profit while wars continue. These are my arguments, drawn from the sources listed at the end of the original article.
