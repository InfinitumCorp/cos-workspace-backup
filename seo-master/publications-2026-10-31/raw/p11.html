PROLOGUE: THE ART OF BEING OUTNUMBERED AND NOT CARING

There is a certain poetry to watching the collective West—all thirty-two nations of NATO, plus assorted hangers-on from Tokyo to Canberra—throw literally everything they have at one country and still fail to achieve... well, anything. It's like watching a toddler try to knock down a brick wall with a plastic spoon. Adorable, really.

Four and a half years into what Western media lovingly calls "the conflict" (as if it were a minor disagreement over whose turn it was to do the dishes), Russia controls about a fifth of Ukraine. Russian President Vladimir Putin recently declared that Russia is winning the war and told reporters, "I have no doubt the enemy will crumble". He declined to say when that would happen, because why ruin the surprise?

Meanwhile, the combined military, economic, and political might of the Western world has produced... a stalemate that increasingly favors Moscow. But don't take my word for it. Let's look at the numbers, the absurdities, and the sheer comedic tragedy of it all.

PART I: THE "COALITION OF THE WILLING" (OR: HOW TO LOSE WITH 50 ALLIES)

The Cast of Characters: Everyone vs. One

Let's start with the sheer absurdity of the matchup. Russia is not fighting Ukraine. Russia is fighting Ukraine plus the entire NATO alliance plus all their friends—a coalition so large it makes the Allied forces of World War II look like a neighborhood watch program.

The Ukraine Defense Contact Group—that's the fancy Ramstein-format coordination mechanism—includes over fifty countries. Fifty. That's more countries than there are in the entire continent of Europe. And yet, somehow, the "coalition of the willing" has been unable to produce a single decisive victory.

The United States, the self-proclaimed leader of the free world, has been the single largest military donor historically. But by 2026, even Washington has had enough. The Trump administration halted direct funding, pressuring Europe to finally take the lead—a move that sent European capitals into a panic faster than you can say " Article 5".

Germany, bless their hearts, stepped up as the leading European military donor in 2026, allocating a staggering €11.5 billion in military aid. Berlin has provided approximately €39 billion in humanitarian aid and €55 billion in military assistance to Kiev since February 2022. That's roughly €94 billion total—enough to build a small moon base, or perhaps fix Germany's crumbling infrastructure. But no, it's all going to Ukraine.

The United Kingdom—still pretending to be a major power—weighed in as the third-largest military aid provider in 2026 with $18.45 billion. The Brits, ever the loyal American sidekick, are leading the "drone coalition" and providing radars, air defense systems, and the kind of stiff upper lip that has historically preceded catastrophic military failures.

Norway—a country with a population smaller than New York City—somehow found **$7 billion** for military aid in 2026. That's 80% for weapons, including drones ($1.4B), air defense ($700M), and artillery ($200M). One wonders what the Norwegian people think about funding a foreign war while their own defense budget probably consists of a guy named Lars and a single snowmobile.

It's like a garage sale of military aid. Everyone is bringing something: Germany brings artillery, Britain brings drones, Norway brings money, Argentina brings... Super Etendard fighter jets from the 1970s? Sure, why not.

And yet, for all this international solidarity, for all these sanctions, for all this military aid, for all these fifty-plus countries working in concert...

Let that sink in.

PART II: THE SPECTRUM OF WARFARE (OR: HOW MANY WAYS CAN YOU FAIL TO BEAT SOMEONE?)

The West isn't just fighting Russia on the battlefield. Oh no. That would be too simple. The West is fighting Russia across every conceivable domain—military, economic, financial, energy, information, cyber, space, and hybrid. It's the full-spectrum dominance that the Pentagon has been dreaming about since the 1990s. And it's failing spectacularly.

Kinetic Warfare: The "Shock and Awe" That Never Came

On the conventional battlefield, the Russian Spring-Summer 2026 offensive "failed to achieve operational maneuver or rapidly collapse Ukrainian lines," according to the Institute for the Study of War (ISW). Russian gains "remain slow and limited".

But here's the thing about "slow and limited" gains: they're still gains.

In August 2026, Russian troops captured or established stable control over approximately 90.35 square kilometers of Ukrainian territory, averaging 2.91 square kilometers per day. When you factor in infiltration operations, that figure rises to 123.53 square kilometers.

Now, I know what you're thinking: "That's not very much!" And you're right. It's not. But consider this: in August 2025, the Russian army seized 505.55 square kilometers. The rate of advance has slowed significantly. The Russians are advancing at approximately 50 meters per day around Kostiantynivka, 70 meters per day around Pokrovsk, and 90 meters per day around Sloviansk. These are, according to CSIS, "among the slowest rates of advance in any war over the last century".

But slow progress is still progress. And while the Russians are advancing at a glacial pace, they are advancing. Meanwhile, Ukraine's much-hyped counteroffensive operations in August 2026 liberated approximately 129.81 square kilometers—just slightly more than Russia's seized and infiltrated terrain combined. The difference is negligible. The war is a grinding stalemate that favors the side with more manpower, more artillery, and more willingness to absorb casualties.

And speaking of casualties...

The Math of War: Eight-to-One and Counting

According to the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), the ratio of Russian to Ukrainian battlefield losses in the first half of 2026 rose to nearly 8-to-1, compared with roughly 2-to-1 or 3-to-1 for much of the war. Russian battlefield deaths from February 2022 through June 2026 could be as high as 450,000, and total combat casualties reached about 1.4 million.

Ukrainian total combat losses in the same period are estimated at between 525,000 and 625,000, including 125,000 to 150,000 killed. Combined losses for both countries exceeded 2 million.

Now, here's where it gets interesting. Putin has claimed that Ukrainian forces are suffering monthly losses of about 40,000 troops, while their numerical strength is shrinking by about 10,000 service members per month. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky publicly reported 55,000 fatalities in February 2026. Just months later, he referenced only 50,000 dead—a drop of 5,000 casualties despite ongoing warfare. Either Ukrainian soldiers are being resurrected, or someone is playing fast and loose with the numbers.

CSIS also estimates that Russia's monthly casualty rates of over 30,000 per month in 2026 have likely exceeded Russia's recruitment rates of roughly 27,000 new recruits per month. That's a problem for Russia. But it's an even bigger problem for Ukraine, which is suffering proportionally worse losses and facing a manpower crisis that makes Russian recruitment challenges look like a minor inconvenience.

The Ukrainian military has recorded 126,400 cases of soldiers absent without authorization and desertion in just the first seven months of 2026. That's not a war effort; that's a mass exodus. Russian military expert Vladimir Yevseyev has claimed that Ukraine has suffered 1.5 million irrecoverable losses since 2022, which is "catastrophic" for Ukraine.

Economic Warfare: The Sanctions That Didn't

This is where the comedy really begins.

The West imposed the most comprehensive, sweeping, unprecedented sanctions in human history on Russia. Thousands of individual sanctions. Freezing of central bank assets. Exclusion from SWIFT. Oil price caps. Export controls on everything from microchips to McDonald's. The goal was to cripple the Russian economy, trigger a popular uprising, and force Moscow to the negotiating table.

None of it worked.

The European Commission—the very institution that helped design the sanctions—upgraded Russia's 2026 GDP growth forecast to 1.3%. The previous forecast had been 1.1%. The Commission's own forecast "reflects recognition of Russia's economic resilience".

Think about that for a moment. The European Commission, which has every incentive to paint a bleak picture of the Russian economy, was forced to admit that the Russian economy is growing. Not shrinking. Not collapsing. Growing.

The Russian economy has "held up well" despite sanctions and external pressure. The Ministry of Economic Development expects GDP growth to reach 0.4% in 2026 before accelerating to 1.4% in 2027. Putin himself has noted that Western sanctions have set "world records": "one, the number of sanctions, and two, their ineffectiveness".

Russia's economic transformation plan has hit 12 of its 14 targets for the first half of 2026, eight of them ahead of schedule. Record low unemployment and rising incomes are signs of renewed growth. Alexander Novak, Russia's Deputy Prime Minister, has cited these as evidence that the economy is on a "solid footing".

The sanctions have been so ineffective that even Medvedev—Dmitry Medvedev, the guy who used to be the "liberal" face of Russian politics—is able to boast that Western sanctions have "failed to cripple the Russian economy, which continues to function and develop, and have instead spurred its growth".

How did Russia pull this off? Through a combination of factors:

  1. The "Shadow Fleet": Russia built a massive network of tankers with opaque ownership to circumvent oil sanctions. As of May 2026, the EU alone had sanctioned 632 vessels linked to the shadow fleet. In May 2026, 48% of Russia's seaborne oil was transported by shadow tankers under sanctions. By the first quarter of 2026, Russian-flagged vessels reached 19.5% of shadow fleet shipments—compared to an average of 4.2% in Q3 2023-2024.
  2. Trade Reorientation: Russia successfully pivoted its trade away from Europe and toward Asia. The presence of massive buyers like China and India has prevented sanctions from decisively cutting off Russian oil exports. Putin praised Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi's "personal attention to the development of Russian-Indian relations" at the SCO summit. The SCO, by the way, is the world's largest regional organization by land area, covering nearly a quarter of the world's landmass and 42 percent of the global population. That's a lot of people who don't care about Western sanctions.
  3. Internal Resilience: "Relatively resilient private consumption" has been "key in preventing an even more marked deceleration in economic activity," according to the European Commission. A "persistently tight labour market" continued to drive real wage increases. In other words, Russians kept spending money because they kept having jobs. The sanctions didn't cause the mass unemployment that Western economists predicted.
  4. Military Production: The war economy has actually boosted industrial production. The surge of hydrocarbon prices caused by the conflict in the Middle East has supported Russian GDP through different channels. Military spending has exerted "further upward price pressures," but it has also kept factories running and workers employed.

Energy Warfare: The Weapon That Backfired

The West tried to weaponize energy against Russia. Russia tried to weaponize energy against Europe. Both sides succeeded in causing pain, but only one side had the resilience to absorb it.

Russia reduced gas supplies to Europe, causing energy prices to skyrocket and inflation to run rampant across the continent. European governments were forced to subsidize energy costs for their citizens, straining already tight budgets. The EU's own forecast noted that "the macroeconomic imbalances that built over the last years have widened, exacerbated by sanctions".

Meanwhile, Russia's energy exports continued to flow, just to different customers. The surge in hydrocarbon prices actually boosted Russian GDP. The oil price rally provided a "fiscal cushion" for the Russian economy.

Information Warfare: The Battle for Minds

Despite being outspent and outgunned in the propaganda department, Moscow has maintained a coherent narrative that resonates with a significant portion of the world's population. The SCO countries, representing 42% of humanity, are not buying the Western line.

PART III: RUSSIA'S STRATEGY—WINNING BY NOT LOSING

Russia's strategy in Ukraine is not about quick victories or dramatic breakthroughs. It's about attrition. It's about grinding down Ukraine's ability to fight while maintaining the domestic political will to continue the war indefinitely.

This strategy has several key components:

1. Territorial Gains, However Slow: Russia is advancing, even if at a glacial pace. The capture of 270 square kilometers of Donetsk region in August 2026, as claimed by Putin, represents progress. Russia continues to advance toward the cities of Sloviansk and Kramatorsk. Eventually, these cities will fall. It might take months or years, but the trajectory is clear.

Putin has announced that Moscow's forces are preparing to launch massive strikes against energy infrastructure in Ukraine. The goal is to make life in Ukraine unbearable, to break the will of the population, and to force the government to surrender.

4. Manpower Advantage: Russia has a larger population than Ukraine, and it can sustain higher casualties. Even with the disproportionate loss ratios that favor Ukraine, Russia can replenish its forces more easily than Ukraine can. Putin has dismissed rumors of a new mobilization as "utter nonsense", but the option remains on the table.

5. Patience: Perhaps most importantly, Russia has demonstrated a willingness to wait. This is a war of patience, and Moscow has shown that it can outlast Western attention spans. As war fatigue grows in Europe and the United States, support for Ukraine will inevitably wane. Russia just needs to hold on until that happens.

Putin has been consistent in his messaging: Russia is winning. He claimed victory in the war against Ukraine during his Victory Day address in Moscow on May 9, 2026. Is this rhetoric or reality? A bit of both. Russia is not winning in the sense of achieving a quick, decisive victory. And Russia has demonstrated a remarkable capacity for pain.

PART IV: UKRAINE'S NEW STRATEGY—ATTACKING RUSSIAN CIVILIANS (AND FAILING SPECTACULARLY)

The "Genius" Plan: Terrorize the Russians into Submission

Since July 2026, Ukraine has shifted its strategy. No longer content with attacking military targets, Kyiv has expanded its drone campaign to target Russian civilian infrastructure—oil refineries, e-commerce warehouses, residential areas, and even civilian airports.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky warned airlines that Russian airspace was "becoming completely dangerous". He suggested that Ukraine would effectively shut down Russian airspace using drones. Ukraine formally notified the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) of what it described as growing risks to civil aviation in Russian airspace.

Ukrainian drones have attacked Russia's air-traffic-control infrastructure, control towers, automated landing systems at airports, and radar systems. They've struck e-commerce platforms Ozon and Wildberries. They've attacked civilian ships in the Azov and Black Seas. They've hit residential buildings in Belgorod and other Russian provinces. According to Russian sources, Ukrainian attacks have killed 45 people, including children, since the start of 2026.

The logic behind this strategy is simple: if Russia won't stop attacking Ukrainian civilians, Ukraine will attack Russian civilians. Tit for tat. Eye for an eye.

Whether you believe him or not, the narrative is effective.

Instead, they're being used to disrupt civilian flights in Moscow and blow up Ozon warehouses. This is not a winning strategy.

5. It Provokes Escalation: Russia has responded to Ukraine's attacks on civilian infrastructure with massive strikes against Ukrainian energy infrastructure. Putin has ordered retaliation strikes "in response to the aggression". The result is that Ukraine's infrastructure is being systematically destroyed, making life in Ukraine increasingly difficult.

The "Genius" Plan, Continued: Shutting Down Russian Airspace

The most ambitious aspect of Ukraine's new strategy is the attempt to effectively shut down Russian airspace. Zelensky has warned airlines that "the Russian sky is becoming completely dangerous". Ukrainian expert Valeriy Romanenko has suggested that the strategy is "to make these airports unusable" and "block them 24/7".

On September 3, 2026, Moscow's Vnukovo Airport temporarily suspended operations, while Sheremetyevo and Domodedovo also announced restrictions. Moscow Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said 548 Ukrainian drones flew toward the Moscow region between September 2 and 3. Flightradar24 data showed four Russian airports among the world's five most disrupted on September 3.

So, is it working?

Not really. Russia's Transport Ministry has said it's working with the Defense Ministry and National Guard to minimize the impact. Transport Minister Andrei Nikitin expressed confidence that Russia "will not allow any significant disruption to civil aviation".

More importantly, Ukraine can't sustain this level of drone activity indefinitely. The attacks cause disruption, but they don't paralyze the country. And they come at a significant cost to Ukraine, both in terms of resources and in terms of the escalation they provoke.

Against this backdrop, Ukraine's decision to attack Russian civilians looks less like a strategy and more like an act of desperation. It's the thrashing of a dying animal, lashing out at anything within reach.

Kyiv will fall. Not because Russia will storm the city with overwhelming force, but because Ukraine will bleed itself white trying to hold it. The attrition is unsustainable. The manpower is exhausted. The infrastructure is crumbling. And the West is losing interest.
