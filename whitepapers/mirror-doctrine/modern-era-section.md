# 6. The modern flare: the feed is part of the escalation system

## Social media and continuous news compress decision time

The modern battlefield has an informational rear area, but not an informational sanctuary. A local event can become a global symbol before investigators establish its basic facts. News organizations, governments, platforms, activists, ordinary users, and armed groups operate on overlapping clocks. They do not share one command structure.

Continuous coverage can increase the political salience of an event and expand fear beyond the place of immediate danger. Repetition is not independent confirmation, however. A widely circulated clip can create apparent evidentiary mass without adding another witness. The analytical risk is that the pressure to appear decisive replaces the time required to decide well.

The inference is not that journalists are collaborators or that reporting violence should be suppressed. Independent reporting can expose misconduct and correct falsehoods. The point is that attention has strategic consequences even when participants have different intentions. A state response and an armed group's original act can become parts of the same circulating narrative.

## ISIS: visibility is not the same as consent

ISIS used social platforms to disseminate propaganda and support recruitment. RAND's study of Arabic Twitter examined both supporters and opponents, finding that opponents were more numerous in the studied environment while supporters posted more intensively. The report also notes that publicized atrocities could galvanize opposition (Bodine-Baron et al., 2016).

This evidence complicates the familiar assertion that an organization “wins” because its content is widely seen. Reach, approval, mobilization, and behavioral effect are not interchangeable. A horrifying image may intimidate one audience, alienate another, and encourage resistance in a third. Counting exposure alone cannot establish which effect predominates.

The mirror temptation is to answer spectacle with superior spectacle. The strategic question is different: does the response reduce the organization's practical capacity and public appeal, or help make its preferred conflict frame unavoidable? This section describes the question, not methods for creating recruitment or intimidation material.

## PSYOP: influence is broader than deception

Western psychological operations belong in the comparison, but not as an automatic synonym for terrorism or disinformation. A U.S. Army institutional history credits the 4th Psychological Operations Group with supporting coalition operations and encouraging surrender during Desert Shield and Desert Storm. That is an official account, with the limitations of institutional self-description, not independent proof of a precise causal effect (U.S. Army, 2009).

The example matters because psychological influence can pursue behavior that reduces further fighting. The category therefore cannot be defined solely by how frightening a message is. Purpose, audience, authority, factual integrity, and the relationship between communication and conduct all matter.

Nor should the psychological effect of any military action automatically be labeled a deliberate psychological operation. Effects can be incidental, unplanned, or contrary to intent. Analytical precision is more useful than treating every persuasive act as evidence of an invisible master plan.

## Reflexive Control Theory and EchoForm™

Reflexive Control Theory concerns the grounds on which another actor reaches a decision. Chotikul's Naval Postgraduate School study reproduces Lefebvre and Smolyan's formulation of reflexive control as transferring grounds for decision-making between opponents. The relevant account appears on printed pages 83–84; the history of Lefebvre's formalization is discussed on printed page 89 (Chotikul, 1986).

The central distinction is between ordering an opponent to act and shaping the premises from which he concludes that the act is his own best option. This is a theoretical mechanism. Evidence that a decision resembles an opponent's preference is not enough to prove successful reflexive control; intent, transmission, reception, and alternative explanations remain to be investigated.

The Finnish National Defence University’s notice for Antti Vasara’s 2020 study places the theory within contemporary Russian military-strategy debate and describes constructive and destructive measures. That institutional description documents an ongoing analytical tradition; it is not proof that any specific recent event was a successful application (National Defence University, 2020).

Russian and Soviet theoretical lineage is relevant, but the concept should not become an ethnic explanation for deception or a label attached to every Russian statement. Nor should older wartime deception automatically be called an application of a theory formalized later. Similarity is not proof of intellectual descent.

In *EchoForm™: Modern Applications of Reflexive Control Theory*, I distinguish the classical grounds-transfer mechanism from the modern informational condition in which it may operate. Its canonical definition concerns a deliberately constructed informational environment intended to produce and return a preferred decision through reinforcing channels. Ordinary repetition or spontaneous agreement alone does not establish an EchoForm™. It is my interpretive framework, not an independently validated substitute for the original literature (Turner, 2026).

The Adversarial Mirror Ratchet™ concerns the response loop that can follow. A state or organization may sincerely conclude that escalation is unavoidable after encountering mutually reinforcing signals. If those signals exaggerate the opponent's strength, erase alternatives, or equate restraint with surrender, the actor can experience a narrowing of choice without receiving a direct command. That is an analytical possibility, not an attribution to a specific contemporary campaign.

## Algorithms, outrage, and the limits of the evidence

Brady and colleagues find that social feedback and network norms can reinforce expressions of moral outrage. Their evidence includes observational Twitter studies and controlled experiments. Importantly, their conclusions concern expressions, not direct measurement of inner emotion or proof of offline violence (Brady et al., 2021).

Algorithmic selection and human learning are related but distinct. Recommendation systems affect exposure; users interpret and reward what they encounter. Neither process guarantees persuasion. Guess and colleagues' experiment replacing algorithmic feeds with chronological feeds during the 2020 U.S. election substantially changed platform experiences but did not significantly change several measured political attitudes over the study period. The publisher record includes later corrections, reinforcing the need for bounded interpretation (Guess et al., 2023; publisher correction notices).

Together, these studies support a restrained conclusion: platform design can shape exposure and expression, but downstream belief and behavior require separate evidence. “The algorithm made them do it” is not an adequate causal account.

The modern ratchet is thus a hypothesis about interacting incentives. Violent events generate attention; attention generates visible expectations; officials and organizations interpret those expectations; responses generate further images. Some participants seek escalation. Others merely seek information, relevance, protection, or recognition. A system can intensify a conflict without every participant intending the aggregate result.

**Let that sink in.** The information environment does not need to convince everyone. It can become dangerous by convincing decision-makers that everyone else already demands the same response.
