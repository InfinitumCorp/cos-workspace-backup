Executive summary
You do not defeat an adaptive opponent by refusing to understand him. You also do not defeat him
merely by becoming him.

“Can’t beat them, join them” contains two different propositions. The first is about learning: adopt an
opponent’s useful organizational insights, recognize his communications environment, and remove
the advantages created by your own rigidity. The second is about abandonment: accept his standards,
copy his abuses, and then exceed them. History supports the necessity of adaptation. It does not es-
tablish the necessity of unlimited imitation.

I call the passage between those propositions the Adversarial Mirror Ratchet™: a reciprocal process
in which actors copy an adversary’s perceived methods, treat the copied method as a new minimum,
and justify further escalation as necessary to recover advantage. The mechanism can operate through
force, intelligence, institutional secrecy, and public communication. Each round can make the next
round appear less exceptional.

The central finding is uncomfortable but clear. Coercion can disrupt organizations and sometimes
contribute to military defeat. That does not demonstrate that cruelty caused success, that it was indis-
pensable, or that the resulting political order is durable. Tactical interruption, organizational destruc-
tion, civilian protection, legality, legitimacy, and reconciliation are different outcomes. A doctrine that
combines them into a single word—victory—conceals its own costs.

Terrorism scholarship makes the mirror especially dangerous. A violent organization may want an op-
ponent to overreact. Provocation can convert state misconduct into recruitment material, dissolve dis-
tinctions between civilians and fighters, and discredit moderates. In that setting, “one-upping” the en-
emy may complete the enemy’s strategy rather than defeat it (Kydd and Walter, 2006).

Social media accelerates this problem. Images travel between battlefield, newsroom, official account,
and private feed. Attention becomes a visible but misleading measure of power. Reflexive Control The-
ory concerns influencing another actor’s decision premises. My EchoForm™ concept examines the
modern informational condition in which those premises are repeatedly reinforced. The mirror
ratchet describes the escalation that can follow when actors mistake the resulting pressure for stra-
tegic necessity.

The doctrinal answer is not passivity. It is discriminating adaptation: learn the mechanism, contest
the advantage, preserve independent judgment, and measure security by more than fear.

1. The problem: when learning becomes
surrender
An enemy does not need to conquer your institutions to change them. He can pressure them into
changing themselves.

A military organization that cannot adapt to dispersed opponents, inexpensive technologies, or unfa-
miliar political networks risks irrelevance. Intelligence services must understand the structures they
investigate. Governments must communicate in the media environments their populations actually
use. None of this requires adopting every method available to an adversary.

Three distinctions matter. Functional mirroring borrows a useful capability: analytical agility, lin-
guistic understanding, institutional coordination, or resilience. Cognitive mirroring models an op-
ponent’s expectations without necessarily sharing them. Normative mirroring adopts his permission
structure: if he disregards a boundary, we may disregard it too. These are this paper’s organizing dis-
tinctions, not established categories attributed to another scholar.

The third is the critical break. A capability can be copied while its application remains constrained. A
permission structure is harder to contain. Once exception becomes precedent, later restraint can be
described as betrayal of earlier sacrifices. Secrecy can protect a legitimate mission, but it can also pre-
vent an organization from discovering that its methods are damaging that mission.

The ratchet is therefore not simply reciprocal violence. It includes institutional memory. Yesterday’s
disputed act becomes today’s inherited option. Political rhetoric, internal incentives, and public ex-
pectations can hold the mechanism in place even after the original emergency changes.

A useful diagnostic separates four questions. What advantage does the adversary actually possess?
Which part of that advantage is transferable? What would borrowing it do to our political objective?
What evidence would show that the borrowed method is making the problem worse? A demand to
mirror “every movement” skips all four.

The stronger version of the counter-escalation thesis deserves a fair hearing. Adversaries can exploit
predictable restraint, regenerate after setbacks, intimidate witnesses, and manipulate public atten-
tion. States have duties to protect people, not merely to express disapproval. More effective intelli-
gence, lawful coercion, and sustained institutional commitment may be necessary. But “more effect-
ive” does not logically mean “more repugnant.” The burden of proof belongs to the proposed method,
not to the emotional force of the demand.

Key word: independence. Adaptation should recover freedom of action. If copying an enemy makes
your choices increasingly dependent on his provocations, you have acquired his tempo without ac-
quiring strategic control.

2. Foundations: power, conscience, and
political purpose
Clausewitz and Mao: interaction is not a mandate for excess
Clausewitz supplies the underlying dynamic. In the opening chapter of On War, reciprocal exertion
pushes the abstract contest toward extremes. The same chapter then returns war to policy, circum-
stances, and political purpose. Reading only the escalation passage produces a distorted doctrine. Vi-
olence is a means; the political object remains the governing question (Clausewitz, Book I, chapter 1).

Mao’s On Protracted War likewise places armed struggle inside a political and social contest. Time, mo-
bilization, organization, and changing relative conditions matter. His analysis cannot responsibly be
reduced to a command to duplicate an opponent’s behavior. Indeed, strategic adaptation often de-
pends on declining the form of contest an opponent prefers (Mao, 1938).

These traditions support studying interaction and adjusting methods. They do not establish an empir-
ical law that the side willing to commit the ugliest acts necessarily wins. If brutality alone supplied de-
cisive advantage, political organization and public cooperation would require little explanation. Both
bodies of thought give those questions substantial weight.


Machiavelli and Greene: fear has a balance sheet
Machiavelli is routinely compressed into permission for ruthlessness. Chapter XVII of The Prince is
more discriminating: its preference for fear over unreliable affection is accompanied by a warning
against hatred. That distinction matters. Immediate obedience can coexist with a growing desire for
revenge. Fear may change visible behavior while hiding the political liabilities underneath it (Ma-
chiavelli, chapter XVII).

Robert Greene’s The 33 Strategies of War belongs to a different category: popular strategic interpreta-
tion built around historical analogies. Its publisher presents it as a guide to conflict in everyday life. It
is useful here as evidence of the modern appeal of competitive framing, not as peer-reviewed proof
that a historical method will transfer successfully to another setting (Greene, publisher description).

The temptation common to simplified readings of both authors is to confuse domination of an en-
counter with mastery of its consequences. The paper’s claim is narrower: fear is a variable to analyze,
not a substitute for analysis.

Dostoevsky, Nietzsche, and Scripture: the exception judges its
author
In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov’s theory of extraordinary individuals imagines permission to
transgress ordinary moral boundaries for a supposedly higher purpose. The novel then subjects that
theory to conscience, suffering, and the human reality of its consequences. It is not a strategic en-
dorsement of exceptional violence (Dostoevsky, Part III, chapter V).

Nietzsche’s warning in Beyond Good and Evil, aphorism 146, about fighting monsters is relevant pre-
cisely because resemblance can become transformation. It is a philosophical warning, not a quantitat-
ive finding about military effectiveness. The opponent can become the organizing principle of the self
that opposes him.

Biblical texts also resist being flattened into a single doctrine of retaliation. Exodus 21:24 contains the
lex talionis formulation; Matthew 5:38–44 explicitly revisits retaliation and commands love of en-
emies. Romans 12:19–21 rejects personal vengeance and warns against being overcome by evil. These
passages arise in different textual settings. None is a modern targeting directive. Their juxtaposition
exposes the interpretive choice hidden whenever sacred authority is invoked to sanctify escalation
(Bible, King James Version).


Hitler, Stalin, and Eisenhower: different records, different lessons
Hitler’s regime integrated propaganda with persecution and genocide. The United States Holocaust
Memorial Museum documents how familiar prejudices, modern media, and institutional control
helped normalize exclusion and conceal mass murder. This is evidence of catastrophic political mobil-
ization, not a usable model for legitimate security (USHMM, “Nazi Propaganda”).

The Library of Congress’s archival exhibition documents Stalinist repression and the extension of
party-state control through the security apparatus. Such records demonstrate the capacity of institu-
tions to impose terror. They do not make internal coercive reach a reliable proxy for external strategic
judgment, social trust, or moral legitimacy (Library of Congress, “Internal Workings”).

The institutional chronology matters: the KGB was a later Soviet security organization, not the correct
name for Stalin’s NKVD-era apparatus. Historical compression creates false continuity and weakens
analysis.

Eisenhower’s farewell address supplies a contrasting institutional warning. Its concern with the ac-
quisition of unwarranted influence and the need for an informed citizenry concerns how a security
system can reshape the society it exists to protect. The lesson is not that security institutions are unne-
cessary. It is that their permanence and power require deliberate checks (Eisenhower, 1961).

Put together, these texts do not deliver a unanimous command to become the enemy. They describe
interaction, political purpose, fear, self-justification, and institutional danger. The mirror must be ex-
amined from both sides.

3. Why organizations choose terror
Explaining terrorism is not excusing it. Nor does explaining strategic calculation imply that every par-
ticipant acts rationally, that the information available is accurate, or that the strategy succeeds.

Bruce Hoffman’s discussion emphasizes psychological repercussions beyond immediate victims. The
act addresses a wider audience. Terrorism is therefore not only physical violence but a relationship
between violence, political purpose, and interpretation (Hoffman, 1998, excerpt).

Martha Crenshaw distinguishes instrumental calculations from organizational dynamics. In the
former, violence is selected to influence an adversary. In the latter, maintaining the group, status, be-
longing, and internal processes can sustain violence even when its public political objectives are not
advancing. This distinction explains why military pressure alone may not settle the question of organ-
izational persistence (Crenshaw, 1985, NCJRS abstract).

Kydd and Walter identify five strategic logics. Attrition attempts to make a policy too costly. Intimida‐
tion seeks compliance by demonstrating the vulnerability of those who resist. Provocation seeks an
indiscriminate response that shifts support toward the violent organization. Spoiling undermines
trust in negotiations and moderate representatives. Outbidding uses violence to compete with rivals
for leadership and support (Kydd and Walter, 2006).

These mechanisms differ. A response suited to one can aggravate another. Publicly displaying resolve
may reassure an intimidated population if it is accompanied by actual protection. The same display,
when accompanied by indiscriminate harm, can assist provocation. Disrupting a negotiating process
may reward spoilers even when officials describe the interruption as refusing to yield.

Robert Pape’s influential study argues that suicide terrorism followed a coercive strategic logic in the
cases he examined. Its scope must remain visible: a historical dataset of suicide attacks is not all ter-
rorism, and an interpretation of apparent concessions is not proof that terrorism generally works.
Ashworth and colleagues challenge causal inference from selecting cases on the outcome to be ex-
plained. Their criticism matters because policy conclusions can outrun research design (Pape, 2003,
abstract; Ashworth et al., 2008).

Max Abrahms reaches a substantially less favorable assessment of terrorism’s political effectiveness,
associating attacks concentrated on civilian targets with failure to attain stated objectives. His account
emphasizes how civilian victimization can harden perceptions of an organization’s goals. Differences
in cases, objectives, and definitions mean these studies should be placed in dialogue rather than con-
verted into dueling slogans (Abrahms, 2006, abstract).

Historically, implementation has included attacks against civilians, hostage-taking, and spectacular
public violence. The relevant doctrinal point is not technical execution. It is audience structure: the
immediate victim, the constituency claimed by the organization, rival groups, state decision-makers,
and outside observers may receive different messages from the same event.

Media exposure can magnify these messages without making their intended interpretation universal.
Horror can attract sympathizers in one audience and repel potential supporters in another. Organiza-
tional leaders may mistake attention for consent, just as states may mistake silence for legitimacy.

The counter-escalation argument now encounters its hardest test. If the organization seeks provoca-
tion, the state cannot assume that exceeding the organization’s violence increases deterrence. It must
ask whether the response reduces capability while protecting the relationships on which prevention
depends. “They started it” explains anger. It does not establish strategic effect.

4. Historical tests of the counter-
escalation thesis
These cases are not controlled experiments. They involve different political objectives, institutions,
populations, and international settings. They test sweeping claims; they do not isolate a universal
causal coefficient for coercion.


Algeria: the tactical horizon is too short
French counterinsurgency in Algeria is a recurrent reference point for claims that extraordinary pres-
sure produces extraordinary intelligence. Calder Walton’s historical analysis acknowledges that tor-
ture sometimes produced useful short-term information, while describing its expansion from excep-
tion into routine and its damage to relationships with the population (Walton, “Torture and intelli-
gence gathering”).

This matters because a source of information can be consumed by the method used to extract it. A
campaign may interrupt a network while weakening the environment in which credible information,
voluntary cooperation, and political accommodation can develop. Algeria’s eventual independence
makes a simple equation between coercive reach and political success untenable. It does not establish
that any single method alone determined the outcome.

The doctrinal issue is the measurement window. If the assessment ends after an immediate disrup-
tion, costs transferred into future recruitment, public distrust, institutional discipline, or political ne-
gotiations disappear from the ledger. They have not disappeared from the conflict.


Chechnya: imposed control and normalization are different
claims
Human Rights Watch’s Swept Under documents abuses by Russian troops during sweep operations,
while also describing rebel attacks, assassinations, kidnapping, and abuses. Its account of 2001 expli-
citly contrasts official normalization efforts with insecurity, displacement, and damaged civilian trust
(HRW, 2002).

A state confronting armed groups has a genuine security problem. Naming that problem does not re-
solve whether particular responses are lawful or effective. The report’s value for this inquiry is the gap
between administrative claims of normality and conditions that people considered unsafe. A school
reopening or a government office moving does not by itself establish that coercive relationships have
become legitimate governance.

This source is a rights organization’s investigation of a defined period, not a complete explanation of
the conflict’s subsequent trajectory. It cannot establish that all coercion failed or that later changes

Sri Lanka 2009: defeat does not close the ledger
The end of the war in 2009 and the defeat of the LTTE are often used to argue that uncompromising
force can terminate an armed organization’s territorial campaign. This is a serious counterexample to
any claim that military force can never be decisive. But it does not establish that alleged violations
were necessary to that result.

The OHCHR investigation published in 2015 reported reasonable grounds to believe serious violations
were committed by all parties in its period of inquiry. Its findings concerning government forces and
the LTTE must be distinguished from criminal judgments against particular people. The report is an
investigative record, not a completed adjudication (OHCHR, 2015).

Ending organized warfare is consequential. So are the treatment of civilians and detainees, accountab-
ility, and the conditions of the resulting peace. A doctrine can recognize the military outcome while
refusing the unsupported inference that the outcome validates every method associated with it. Ne-
cessity cannot be proved simply by pointing to the winner.


Phoenix: intelligence reach, institutional incentives, and
accountability
A declassified December 1969 memorandum describes the Provincial Reconnaissance Units as part of
the Phoenix/Phung Hoang effort against the covert communist apparatus. Officials credited the pro-
gram with effectiveness while recognizing political risk. The published record also acknowledges
methods described as extreme by American standards and discusses limiting visible American identi-
fication with the program (FRUS, 1969, document 157).

The source is particularly useful because the tension appears inside the official record, not only in
later criticism. Institutional assessments can simultaneously identify disruption and seek distance
from the consequences of the means used. That is not proof that every activity under the program was
unlawful. It is evidence that claimed effectiveness did not settle responsibility.

The mirror here is organizational: a state-backed apparatus seeks to penetrate an underground appar-
atus. The ratchet appears when secrecy and internal production measures become substitutes for ex-
ternal examination. Disabling parts of a political network cannot, on its own, establish that the larger
political order has become sustainable.

Israeli targeted killing policy: legal scrutiny is part of the contest
The Israeli Supreme Court’s 2006 judgment in HCJ 769/02 rejected a blanket conclusion that every tar-
geted killing is prohibited or that every such action is permitted. Its published summary requires ex-
amination of individual circumstances, reliable information, less harmful alternatives where avail-
able, proportionality, and independent retrospective investigation (Israeli Supreme Court, 2006).

This is a legal framework from a specific case, not evidence that a particular policy succeeded every-
where or a substitute for current legal advice. It is also not a judgment about all Mossad operations;
different agencies and legal contexts must not be collapsed together.

For the mirror thesis, its significance is institutional. A state may seek to interrupt future violence
without accepting that an adversary’s conduct erases legal boundaries. The effectiveness question re-
mains empirical: disruption may be temporary, successors may emerge, and civilian harm may
change the political response. A judicial ruling does not answer those questions, but it prevents neces-
sity from being treated as a self-certifying assertion.

The takeaway: These cases demonstrate neither universal failure nor universal success of force. They
demonstrate the inadequacy of “more ruthless” as a complete theory of victory.

5. Secret wars: intelligence is not one
undifferentiated weapon
The names Mossad, KGB, CIA, MI6, and FBI carry reputations that can obscure differences in mission,
jurisdiction, historical period, and evidence. A neutral assessment must resist assembling them into a
fictional common doctrine.

The CIA and British SIS/MI6 involvement in the 1953 Iranian coup illustrates covert intervention
across intelligence, political influence, and state power. National Security Archive documentation
identifies British approaches to the United States and places the joint intervention in its historical re-
cord. The immediate removal of a government is an outcome; the broader political consequences can-
not be reduced to that immediate objective (National Security Archive, 2017).

For the KGB, the AIDS disinformation campaign is a documented example of active measures. Wilson
Center analysis of Soviet-bloc records identifies the promotion of the false allegation that AIDS origin-
ated as an American biological weapon. It also corrects popular confusion over the campaign’s nam-
ing, distinguishing the Stasi designation “Denver” from widespread use of “Infektion” (Selvage and
Nehring, 2019).

This case shows that the deliberate contamination of public knowledge predates contemporary plat-
forms. It does not demonstrate that every later rumor is intelligence-directed. An enduring story can
travel through independent repetition, political incentives, and sincere belief. Its circulation and its
origin require separate evidence.

For Mossad, the capture of Adolf Eichmann offers a different mechanism: clandestine action followed
by a highly public trial. The Holocaust Encyclopedia records his abduction in Argentina and sub-
sequent prosecution in Israel. The case must not be generalized into approval of cross-border abduc-
tion or erased into a story about assassination. Its political meaning included public evidence and leg-
al proceedings, not secrecy alone (USHMM, “Adolf Eichmann”).

J. Edgar Hoover’s FBI supplies a warning about the migration of security logic into domestic political
suppression. The Senate Historical Office’s account of the Church Committee identifies COINTELPRO
as covert action intended to disrupt and discredit groups and individuals. Its subjects included civil-
rights and antiwar organizations and Martin Luther King Jr. The Senate account locates these abuses
within failures of checks and balances, rather than attributing the whole problem to one party or one
individual (U.S. Senate, Church Committee history).

These cases reveal different ways of exercising hidden power: intervention, information contamina-
tion, capture, and disruption. They also reveal different public tests. Who authorized the activity?
What law applied? What could an oversight body verify? Did institutional secrecy preserve a legitimate
capability, or conceal the absence of a defensible boundary?

6. The modern flare: the feed is part of the
escalation system
Social media and continuous news compress decision time
The modern battlefield has an informational rear area, but not an informational sanctuary. A local
event can become a global symbol before investigators establish its basic facts. News organizations,
governments, platforms, activists, ordinary users, and armed groups operate on overlapping clocks.
They do not share one command structure.

Continuous coverage can increase the political salience of an event and expand fear beyond the place
of immediate danger. Repetition is not independent confirmation, however. A widely circulated clip
can create apparent evidentiary mass without adding another witness. The analytical risk is that the
pressure to appear decisive replaces the time required to decide well.

The inference is not that journalists are collaborators or that reporting violence should be suppressed.
Independent reporting can expose misconduct and correct falsehoods. The point is that attention has
strategic consequences even when participants have different intentions. A state response and an
armed group’s original act can become parts of the same circulating narrative.


ISIS: visibility is not the same as consent
ISIS used social platforms to disseminate propaganda and support recruitment. RAND’s study of Arab-
ic Twitter examined both supporters and opponents, finding that opponents were more numerous in
the studied environment while supporters posted more intensively. The report also notes that publi-
cized atrocities could galvanize opposition (Bodine-Baron et al., 2016).

This evidence complicates the familiar assertion that an organization “wins” because its content is
widely seen. Reach, approval, mobilization, and behavioral effect are not interchangeable. A horrify-
ing image may intimidate one audience, alienate another, and encourage resistance in a third. Count-
ing exposure alone cannot establish which effect predominates.

The mirror temptation is to answer spectacle with superior spectacle. The strategic question is differ-
ent: does the response reduce the organization’s practical capacity and public appeal, or help make its
preferred conflict frame unavoidable? This section describes the question, not methods for creating
recruitment or intimidation material.

PSYOP: influence is broader than deception
Western psychological operations belong in the comparison, but not as an automatic synonym for ter-
rorism or disinformation. A U.S. Army institutional history credits the 4th Psychological Operations
Group with supporting coalition operations and encouraging surrender during Desert Shield and
Desert Storm. That is an official account, with the limitations of institutional self-description, not in-
dependent proof of a precise causal effect (U.S. Army, 2009).

The example matters because psychological influence can pursue behavior that reduces further fight-
ing. The category therefore cannot be defined solely by how frightening a message is. Purpose, audi-
ence, authority, factual integrity, and the relationship between communication and conduct all mat-
ter.

Nor should the psychological effect of any military action automatically be labeled a deliberate psy-
chological operation. Effects can be incidental, unplanned, or contrary to intent. Analytical precision
is more useful than treating every persuasive act as evidence of an invisible master plan.


Reflexive Control Theory and EchoForm™
Reflexive Control Theory concerns the grounds on which another actor reaches a decision. Chotikul’s
Naval Postgraduate School study reproduces Lefebvre and Smolyan’s formulation of reflexive control
as transferring grounds for decision-making between opponents. The relevant account appears on
printed pages 83–84; the history of Lefebvre’s formalization is discussed on printed page 89 (Chotikul,
1986).

The central distinction is between ordering an opponent to act and shaping the premises from which
he concludes that the act is his own best option. This is a theoretical mechanism. Evidence that a de-
cision resembles an opponent’s preference is not enough to prove successful reflexive control; intent,
transmission, reception, and alternative explanations remain to be investigated.

The Finnish National Defence University’s notice for Antti Vasara’s 2020 study places the theory within
contemporary Russian military-strategy debate and describes constructive and destructive measures.
That institutional description documents an ongoing analytical tradition; it is not proof that any spe-
cific recent event was a successful application (National Defence University, 2020).

Russian and Soviet theoretical lineage is relevant, but the concept should not become an ethnic ex-
planation for deception or a label attached to every Russian statement. Nor should older wartime de-
ception automatically be called an application of a theory formalized later. Similarity is not proof of
intellectual descent.

In EchoForm™: Modern Applications of Reflexive Control Theory, I distinguish the classical grounds-trans-
fer mechanism from the modern informational condition in which it may operate. Its canonical defin-
ition concerns a deliberately constructed informational environment intended to produce and return
a preferred decision through reinforcing channels. Ordinary repetition or spontaneous agreement

The Adversarial Mirror Ratchet™ concerns the response loop that can follow. A state or organization

surrender, the actor can experience a narrowing of choice without receiving a direct command. That
is an analytical possibility, not an attribution to a specific contemporary campaign.


Algorithms, outrage, and the limits of the evidence
Brady and colleagues find that social feedback and network norms can reinforce expressions of moral
outrage. Their evidence includes observational Twitter studies and controlled experiments. Import-
antly, their conclusions concern expressions, not direct measurement of inner emotion or proof of
offline violence (Brady et al., 2021).

Algorithmic selection and human learning are related but distinct. Recommendation systems affect
exposure; users interpret and reward what they encounter. Neither process guarantees persuasion.
Guess and colleagues’ experiment replacing algorithmic feeds with chronological feeds during the
2020 U.S. election substantially changed platform experiences but did not significantly change several
measured political attitudes over the study period. The publisher record includes later corrections, re-
inforcing the need for bounded interpretation (Guess et al., 2023; publisher correction notices).

Together, these studies support a restrained conclusion: platform design can shape exposure and ex-
pression, but downstream belief and behavior require separate evidence. “The algorithm made them
do it” is not an adequate causal account.

The modern ratchet is thus a hypothesis about interacting incentives. Violent events generate atten-
tion; attention generates visible expectations; officials and organizations interpret those expectations;
responses generate further images. Some participants seek escalation. Others merely seek informa-
tion, relevance, protection, or recognition. A system can intensify a conflict without every participant
intending the aggregate result.

Let that sink in. The information environment does not need to convince everyone. It can become
dangerous by convincing decision-makers that everyone else already demands the same response.

7. What a serious doctrine must measure
A doctrine should survive the removal of its most dramatic example.

The state counter-escalation thesis is strongest when it argues that credible protection requires sus-
tained capacity and cannot depend on an opponent’s goodwill. It is weakest when it turns that argu-
ment into immunity from scrutiny. A state does not have to choose between helplessness and unlim-
ited imitation.

Assessment should separate immediate disruption from longer-term regeneration; visible obedience
from voluntary cooperation; communications reach from persuasion; territorial control from safe ci-
vilian life; and institutional capability from accountable authority. These distinctions do not assume a
particular answer. They make the answer testable.

Several confounders recur. An organization may weaken because of internal splits, declining external
support, political accommodation, financial constraints, or changes in public attitudes. Coercion may
coincide with those developments. Coincidence is not proof that an abusive method produced the res-
ult. Conversely, a lawful policy’s failure in one setting does not prove restraint itself caused failure.

The missing counterfactual matters most. “We used this method and later prevailed” leaves un-
answered whether another method would have produced similar or better results at lower cost. “We
restrained ourselves and violence continued” leaves unanswered whether escalation would have in-
tensified the violence. Historical doctrine cannot eliminate uncertainty. It must stop disguising it as
certainty.

The legal floor is not merely another preference to weigh against convenience. Common Article 3 re-
quires humane treatment of persons taking no active part in hostilities and prohibits, among other
acts, torture, hostage-taking, and executions without the required judicial guarantees in the conflicts
to which it applies. An opponent’s breach does not insert an exception into that text (Geneva Conven-
tions, Common Article 3).

Similarly, command responsibility cannot be outsourced to the phrase “the enemy forced our hand.”
Applicable legal standards vary by jurisdiction and conflict, but institutional responsibility requires
lawful orders, credible supervision, preservation of evidence, and investigation of alleged violations. A
body of doctrine that treats those functions as obstacles is confusing freedom from review with opera-
tional freedom.

The strategic balance sheet should also count institutional damage. A method that rewards unreliable
reporting can degrade intelligence. A communications apparatus that penalizes uncertainty can make
officials less informed. An emergency power without meaningful review can outlive its original pur-
pose. These are risks to examine, not inevitable consequences of every security measure.

8. Recommendations: adapt without
surrendering the standard
The practical recommendations here concern governance and defensive resilience, not the execution
of violence or influence operations.

1. Preserve the political objective. Define the condition the campaign is supposed to produce in
terms people can recognize: protection, lawful order, reduced coercion, and a durable settlement
where attainable. Do not substitute an opponent’s suffering for a description of success. For a mid-size
organization, the parallel is service continuity and trustworthy decisions—not humiliating an attacker.

2. Mirror understanding before method. Develop the language, contextual knowledge, and analytical
capacity needed to understand the adversary. Examine why a method appears effective before borrow-
ing its visible form. Organizations should study incidents and attacker incentives without copying
criminal behavior or normalizing unauthorized testing.

3. Keep capability and permission separate. A faster analytical process or more resilient communic-
ations system can be adopted without importing an opponent’s rules. Require explicit boundaries and
independent review. Corporate incident teams need the same separation between technically possible
action and authorized action.

4. Protect the information supply. Distinguish verified facts, provisional assessments, adversary
claims, and analytic inference. Preserve original evidence and corrections. Decision-makers should
have an independent second source and out-of-band verification for consequential claims, especially
when a feed makes an emergency appear unanimous.

5. Measure outcomes beyond attention. Communications evaluation should not equate impressions
with persuasion or anger with support. Security evaluation should not equate activity with prevention.
Mid-size organizations should track recovery, validated incidents, false positives, and confidence in
essential services, not merely the number of alerts generated.

6. Budget for persistence and resilience. Cheap provocations can impose expensive reactions. Lay-
ers, redundancy, protected personnel, and the ability to continue functioning may offer more durable
value than a spectacular response. The rear area includes vendors, platforms, administrative pro-
cesses, and the people making decisions under fatigue.

7. Re‐test the countermeasure and its costs. A response that once reduced risk may decay or create a
new vulnerability. Schedule review, protect dissenting analysis, and retain a route back from emer-
gency measures. Pre-authorized defensive decisions should remain bounded, documented, and re-
versible where possible.

Conclusion: defeat the dependency
“Can’t beat them, join them” is useful as a question. As an unconditional doctrine, it gives the ad-
versary too much authority.

You must understand the enemy’s methods. You may need to outmatch his speed, persistence, intelli-
gence, and capacity to adapt. But if he selects the moral floor and you agree only to go lower, he is still
setting the terms.

The Adversarial Mirror Ratchet™ names that loss of independence. Terrorism scholarship explains
why overreaction can be sought rather than merely endured. Historical cases show why immediate
coercive results must be distinguished from legality, legitimacy, and durable political outcomes. Echo-
Form™ and Reflexive Control Theory clarify why the perceived necessity of escalation deserves as
much scrutiny as the escalation itself.

The modern feed adds speed, repetition, and visible social pressure. It does not repeal uncertainty.
Nor does it transform cruelty into strategy.

The strongest doctrine is not “become the enemy, then become worse.” It is: understand the enemy
deeply enough to prevent him from becoming the author of your decisions.

Let’s stay engaged. Dialogue is key.

Glossary
Adversarial Mirror Ratchet™: William Aaron Turner’s proposed term for reciprocal copying, normal-
ization, and escalation of an adversary’s perceived methods.

EchoForm™: Turner’s framework for a deliberately constructed, reinforcing informational environ-
ment surrounding a preferred decision; related to, but distinct from, classical reflexive control.

Reflexive Control Theory: A theoretical tradition examining transfer of decision grounds so that an-
other actor’s reasoning produces a preferred decision.

Provocation: A strategic logic seeking an adversary’s response that shifts political support toward the
initiating organization.

Outbidding: Competition in which violent action signals commitment relative to rival groups.

PSYOP: Psychological operations; deliberate influence activity, not a synonym for every psychological
effect, deception, or terrorism.

Legitimacy: Perceived rightfulness of authority; distinct from the capacity to compel visible compli-
ance.
