# 2. Theoretical and Historical Foundations

## 2.1 Irregular Warfare Principles

Classical irregular warfare emphasizes population-centric operations, guerrilla tactics, intelligence-driven targeting, and the erosion of an adversary's will and legitimacy. Mao, Galula, and modern special operations doctrine all stress that the decisive terrain is human cognition and social cohesion. Corporate Cognitive Warfare imports these principles into markets where attention, trust, and narrative legitimacy are the scarce resources.

The U.S. Department of Defense's 2020 summary of its Irregular Warfare Annex describes irregular warfare as a struggle among state and non-state actors to influence populations and affect legitimacy, favoring indirect and asymmetric approaches (U.S. DoD 2020). Two elements of that description carry straight into commerce. The first is *legitimacy*. In a market, legitimacy is the belief that a company has the right to be chosen: it is credible, it is trustworthy, it belongs in the consideration set. The second is *indirect approach*. Irregular actors rarely meet a stronger opponent head-on. They change the terms of the contest instead.

Mao's writings on guerrilla warfare, translated by Samuel B. Griffith and reprinted by the U.S. Marine Corps as FMFRP 12-18, stress the guerrilla's dependence on the population and the importance of choosing when and where to engage (Mao 1961/1989). David Galula's *Counterinsurgency Warfare: Theory and Practice* (1964) approaches the same problem from the other side and treats the population's support as the objective of the whole contest. A corporate strategist does not need to adopt either author's politics to see the point: the side with the people's trust can operate where the other cannot.

Four irregular-warfare principles translate into corporate practice:

- **The population is the objective.** Market share follows belief. Campaigns that win clicks but lose trust are tactical victories and strategic defeats.
- **Intelligence precedes action.** Irregular forces survive on superior knowledge of local terrain. In commerce, that means knowing the audience's language, fears, informal leaders and information habits better than the competitor does.
- **Concentrate where the opponent is weak.** A challenger should fight in the segment, channel or narrative the incumbent neglects, not where the incumbent's budget is strongest.
- **Legitimacy is the center of gravity.** An irregular actor that loses legitimacy loses its base. A company caught deceiving loses customers, partners, regulators and staff at the same moment.

## 2.2 From Military Cognitive Warfare to the Boardroom

Military cognitive warfare seeks to degrade an opponent's decision-making, cohesion, and willingness to contest. In the corporate domain, comparable effects on competitors' and audiences' decisions can be achieved, lawfully, through narrative framing, selective amplification of true information, algorithmic awareness, and precision influence. The tools of digital marketing—micro-targeting, A/B testing, emotional narrative design, and coordinated amplification—share their technical substrate with modern influence operations. That shared substrate is exactly why companies need both an offensive discipline bounded by law and ethics and a defensive discipline that recognizes when the same tools are turned against them.

NATO's scientific community has made cognitive warfare a formal research area. A 2020 study for NATO Allied Command Transformation's Innovation Hub argued that the human mind should be treated as an operational domain in its own right (du Cluzel 2020). The NATO Chief Scientist's research report on cognitive warfare goes further and summarizes NATO's recent definition: cognitive warfare is "the fight for Cognitive Superiority," in which contesting the environment comprises "deliberate, synchronized military and non-military activities throughout the continuum of competition designed to gain, maintain and protect cognitive advantage" (NATO STO n.d.). Readers of the original edition of this paper will recognize the parallel with its definition of Corporate Cognitive Warfare as the effort to "gain, maintain, and protect cognitive superiority." The same report describes three functions for research on cognitive warfare: to degrade adversaries' capabilities to influence, to improve human and technological cognition, and to be resilient, "withstanding and recovering" performance in the face of cognitive threats (NATO STO n.d.).

For a company, those three functions map to three questions. Can we reduce the effect of hostile or misleading narratives about us? Can we improve how our own people sense and decide? Can we absorb a cognitive attack, such as a viral falsehood, and recover quickly? Sections 6 and 8 are organized around those questions.

## 2.3 A Note on Method and Neutrality

The next three subsections describe how different military traditions conceptualize influence over perception and decision-making. Three cautions apply.

First, these are doctrinal and analytic concepts, described here from published sources. The paper does not assess whether any state has used them, how well, or to what effect, and it leaves out accounts of specific operations.

Second, much of the English-language literature on Russian and Chinese concepts is written by analysts working in or for Western defense institutions, often from translated sources. Some of those analysts themselves warn against over-reading. Keir Giles notes that the phrase "reflexive control" is "far more frequently encountered in recent Western writing about Russian information warfare principles than in original Russian sources" (Giles 2016). Peter Mattis argues that the "three warfares" are better understood as part of the Party's broader political work than as a narrowly military doctrine (Mattis 2018). Nathan Beauchamp-Mustafaga finds Chinese military thinking on psychological warfare "much more diverse than previously understood" (Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2023). Where such caveats exist, this paper reports them.

Third, the paper uses these concepts as lenses. What matters for a company is the underlying logic—how decisions can be shaped by the information environment around them—and what that logic implies for lawful competition and for defense.

## 2.4 Russian Reflexive Control Theory

### Origins

Reflexive control began as a mathematical and psychological theory before it became a military one. Its originator is generally identified as Vladimir A. Lefebvre, a Soviet researcher who worked within the logic of "reflexive games": models in which each party reasons about the other's reasoning, including the other's picture of itself (Vasara 2020; Thomas 2015). Lefebvre's 1967 book, published in Moscow, was translated into English as *Conflicting Structures* (Lefebvre 2015), and his work on reflexive systems appeared in English as *The Structure of Awareness: Toward a Symbolic Language of Human Reflexion* (Lefebvre 1977). Timothy Thomas, a long-time analyst at the U.S. Army's Foreign Military Studies Office (FMSO), records Lefebvre's early definition of reflexive control as "a process by which one enemy transmits the reasons or bases for making decisions to another" (Thomas 2015).

The definition most often cited in Western literature is the one Diane Chotikul gives in her 1986 Naval Postgraduate School study: reflexive control is "a means of conveying to a partner or an opponent specially prepared information to incline him to voluntarily make the predetermined decision" (Chotikul 1986). Thomas uses the same formulation and adds the phrase "desired by the initiator of the action" (Thomas 2015). Chotikul's study is notable for its method. Rather than analyze the mathematics, it sets out to place the theory in its historical and psychocultural context, in order to help Western analysts understand why the concept took the shape it did (Chotikul 1986).

### Development in military thought

Antti Vasara's 2020 study for the Finnish National Defence University traces the theory from Soviet cybernetics into military journals and later Russian strategy. He reports, for example, a 1971 article in the Soviet journal *Voennaya Mysl'* (*Military Thought*) by Major General M. Ionov, which held that an opponent can be persuaded to make decisions favorable to the other side when several conditions are met: the opponent is placed under pressure, influence is exerted on its situational assessment, on its decision-making algorithm and on the way it selects its goal, and influence is exerted on the timing of its decisions (Vasara 2020, citing Ionov 1971). Vasara's model divides reflexive control into *constructive* methods, which build a situational picture that leads the opponent toward a particular decision, and *destructive* methods, which disrupt or overload the opponent's decision process (Vasara 2020).

Thomas's 2004 article in *The Journal of Slavic Military Studies* remains a standard English-language survey of the concept and of the Soviet and Russian military authors who developed it (Thomas 2004). Thomas later cautioned that the most important lesson for students of Russian thought is to study what Russian military theorists actually say, rather than to force Western templates onto it (Thomas 2015). Keir Giles's handbook for the NATO Defense College describes reflexive control as "predetermining an adversary's decision" in one's favor "by altering key factors in the adversary's perception of the world." It compares the technique to "a skilful barrister cross-examining a witness," leading the other side through a series of decisions that successively close off options (Giles 2016).

Later Russian military writing places information and psychological effects at the center of conflict more broadly. In a 2013 article in *Military Thought*, S. G. Chekinov and S. A. Bogdanov wrote that "a new-generation war will be dominated by information and psychological warfare," which "will largely lay the groundwork for victory" (Chekinov and Bogdanov 2013). In a widely cited 2013 article, translated and republished by the U.S. Army's *Military Review* in 2016 "to acquaint our readers with the perspectives of senior Russian military leaders," General Valery Gerasimov wrote that "the role of nonmilitary means of achieving political and strategic goals has grown, and, in many cases, they have exceeded the power of force of weapons in their effectiveness" (Gerasimov 2016). A figure accompanying the translation depicts a 4:1 correlation of nonmilitary to military measures (Gerasimov 2016).

### Lessons for organizations

Stripped of its military context, reflexive control rests on four ideas that are useful to any strategist.

1. **Decisions are made inside a model.** Every decision-maker acts on a picture of the situation, of the options and of the other side. Changing the picture changes the decision.
2. **Model the other side's model.** Lefebvre's reflexive games treat strategy as recursive: I reason about how you reason about me. Competitive strategy that ignores how competitors and customers see the firm is strategy with a blind spot.
3. **The decision process has levers.** Ionov's conditions—pressure, situational assessment, decision rules, goal selection and timing—are a usable checklist for understanding why any audience decides as it does.
4. **The most effective influence feels self-generated.** The target "voluntarily" makes the decision. In commerce, this is the difference between pushing a message and helping a buyer reach a conclusion through evidence that the buyer finds credible.

The lawful corporate version of these ideas is familiar under other names: buyer-journey research, competitive war-gaming, scenario planning, pricing signals and category design. The firm works to understand how a buyer or competitor frames a decision and then supplies true, relevant information, such as a benchmark, a demonstration, a published commitment or a reference customer, that changes the frame. What the commercial version excludes is the "specially prepared" falsehood. A company that feeds a competitor or a market fabricated information crosses from strategy into fraud, market manipulation or false advertising.

The defensive lesson matters just as much. If decisions can be steered by shaping the information around them, every organization's own decision process is an attack surface. Section 6 treats reflexive-control-style provocation, meaning attempts to bait a company into an overreaction that serves someone else, as a threat category. It also sets out "decision hygiene" practices that make an organization harder to steer.

## 2.5 Chinese Cognitive Domain Operations

### The "three warfares"

Analysts of Chinese military thought usually begin with the "three warfares" (三战): public opinion warfare, psychological warfare and legal warfare. Elsa Kania, writing in the Jamestown Foundation's *China Brief*, traces their formal adoption to the 2003 and 2010 Political Work Regulations of the People's Liberation Army (PLA), under the heading of "wartime political work," and to guidelines promulgated in 2005 that incorporated them into education and training (Kania 2016). In her summary, "in peacetime and wartime alike, the application of the three warfares is intended to control the prevailing discourse and influence perceptions" (Kania 2016). From the PLA texts she reviews, Kania concludes that the three warfares constitute a "perceptual preparation of the battlefield" (Kania 2016).

The U.S. Department of Defense's 2023 annual report to Congress describes the three components in parallel terms: public opinion warfare "creates and disseminates information to guide an adversary's public opinion and gain support from domestic and foreign audiences"; psychological warfare "uses propaganda, deception, and coercion to induce pressure and affect the behavior of the target audience"; and legal warfare "uses domestic and international laws to shape narratives" (U.S. DoD 2023). A 2013 study prepared for the Office of Net Assessment by Stefan Halper examined the concept in detail (Halper 2013). Peter Mattis, as noted above, cautions that the three warfares should be read as part of the Party's broader political work rather than as a narrowly military innovation (Mattis 2018).

The PLA Academy of Military Science's 2013 *Science of Military Strategy*, available in English through the China Aerospace Studies Institute's translation series, is one of the authoritative texts that analysts draw on for this material (PLA AMS 2013/2021; Kania 2016).

### From psychological warfare to cognitive domain operations

Since the late 2010s, Chinese military writers have discussed a broader concept: cognitive domain operations (认知域作战). Beauchamp-Mustafaga's 2019 analysis describes it as a "next-generation evolution of psychological warfare" that "seeks to use information to influence an adversary's cognitive functions, spanning from peacetime public opinion to wartime decision-making" (Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2019). The stated goal, in his account, is "mind superiority" (制脑权), which PLA writers present as following sea, air and information superiority as a condition of victory (Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2019). He also notes that the concept was "largely inspired by the U.S. military's emphasis on the cognitive domain's decisive role in modern warfare" (Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2019).

Josh Baughman of the China Aerospace Studies Institute (CASI) at Air University summarizes PLA Daily and Academy of Military Science writings on the subject. He quotes a PLA Daily definition of cognition as "the process by which people acquire, process, and apply information and knowledge," and an AMS formulation that cognitive domain operations "take people's will, belief, thinking, and psychology as direct combat targets, and seek to affect decision-making and actions by changing the opponent's cognition" (Baughman 2023). Baughman also notes that the cognitive domain was "not something uniquely Chinese," tracing the term to a 2001 U.S. Department of Defense report on network-centric warfare (Baughman 2023).

The AMS work Baughman reviews lists eight "operational characteristics." Two are directly relevant to the commercial lens. The first is competition for "Three Powers": the right to define the nature of an event, dominance of the event's process, and the right to judge its outcome (Baughman 2023). The second is the claim that "information moves faster than cannonballs," with social media treated as the main channel through which cognition is shaped (Baughman 2023). Whatever one thinks of the military application, the "Three Powers" are a precise description of what happens in any public controversy, product launch or crisis: someone defines what the event is, someone leads how it unfolds, and someone declares who won.

The 2023 DoD report describes cognitive domain operations as combining psychological warfare with cyber operations and modern communication platforms, "designed to achieve strategic national security goals by affecting a target's cognition," and notes PLA interest in incorporating AI, big data, brain science and neuroscience (U.S. DoD 2023).

### Technology and cognition

Several studies examine the role of emerging technology in this body of thought. Kania's 2019 article in *PRISM*, published by the U.S. National Defense University Press, examines PLA interest in brain science, AI and "human-machine hybrid intelligence" (Kania 2019). A 2021 CNA study on "intelligent warfare" by Kevin Pollpeter and Amanda Kerrigan reports that PLA writers "conclude that the cognitive domain will become more important in intelligent warfare," and that militaries will seek "to influence an adversary's perceptions through the denial, degradation, and manipulation of data and algorithms" (Pollpeter and Kerrigan 2021).

Beauchamp-Mustafaga's 2023 RAND report is the most detailed English-language treatment. Drawing on Chinese military books, journals and patent filings, it surveys PLA interest in big data, brain science and cognitive modeling for psychological warfare (Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2023). Its conclusions are measured. It finds that most of the next-generation capabilities surveyed, if realized, "are unlikely to fundamentally change Chinese strategic behavior." It identifies a different risk: that leaders might *believe* such technologies let them predict or shape adversary decisions, and so act with misplaced confidence (Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2023). That observation generalizes well beyond any one military. Confidence in one's ability to engineer other people's minds is itself a cognitive vulnerability.

### Lessons for organizations

Read neutrally, the Chinese literature contributes four ideas to the corporate lens.

1. **Perception is prepared in advance.** The idea of "perceptual preparation of the battlefield" implies that the contest for belief starts long before any crisis or launch. Companies that build trust, evidence and relationships in calm periods are better placed when contests begin.
2. **Narrative, psychology and rules work together.** The three warfares pair public opinion, psychological pressure and the use of law and rules to frame what is legitimate. The commercial counterpart is the integration of communications, persuasion and standards: industry certifications, benchmarks and regulatory submissions that establish what "good" means in a category.
3. **Contest the "Three Powers" lawfully.** Defining an event, leading its development and judging its outcome describes crisis communications and category leadership. A company does this lawfully by being first with accurate facts, transparent about what it is doing, and consistent in how it reports results.
4. **Beware the overconfidence trap.** RAND's caution about misplaced confidence applies to marketing technology. Psychographic models, attribution dashboards and generative tools can create an illusion of control over audiences who remain free and skeptical.

## 2.6 NATO and Western Concepts

Western militaries have their own lineage of influence doctrine: psychological operations, information operations, strategic communications and, more recently, cognitive warfare as a field of study. NATO's work is notable for its defensive emphasis. The Chief Scientist's report states that cognitive warfare is "broader than InfoOps, PsyOps, STRATCOM, and cyber operations." It also stresses resilience—the ability to withstand and recover—as one of three core functions (NATO STO n.d.). The 2020 Innovation Hub study by François du Cluzel argued for recognizing a "human domain" of operations (du Cluzel 2020).

Taken together, the three traditions show a convergence. Each, in its own terms, treats the mind as contested terrain; each pays close attention to information technology as a multiplier; and each recognizes that influence operates in peacetime as well as in conflict. They differ in emphasis. Reflexive control focuses on steering an opponent's decision process. Cognitive domain operations stress the integration of narrative, psychology, law and technology across society. The NATO research agenda gives particular weight to resilience.

## 2.8 Limits of the Analogy

Military doctrine is a lens, not a license. A company is not a state, its competitors are not enemies, and its customers are not targets in any hostile sense. Four differences constrain how far the analogy goes.

**Consent and choice.** Customers can walk away, and the law protects their right to make informed choices. Commercial influence that removes that choice through deception or manipulation is prohibited.

**Accountability.** Companies operate under consumer protection, competition, privacy, securities and platform rules, and their communications can be audited, litigated and regulated.

**Repeated games.** A company deals with the same customers, employees and regulators again and again. Trust compounds, and so does betrayal. Tactics that might "work" once are ruinous over many rounds.

**Transparency norms.** Much military influence doctrine assumes some secrecy. Commercial influence increasingly assumes the opposite: disclosure of paid relationships, labeling of AI-generated content, and transparency about targeting.

The value of the doctrine lies in its rigor. It forces disciplined thinking about terrain, audiences, decision processes, timing and resilience, and it makes plain what hostile actors may attempt against the organization.
