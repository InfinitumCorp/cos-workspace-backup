# 3. Core Techniques and Tactics

The original paper identified five core techniques and a set of further irregular-warfare adaptations. This section keeps each description and adds, for each technique, its doctrinal logic, detailed lawful applications, the rules that bound it, and how to measure it. In keeping with the paper's ethics statement, wherever a technique has an illegitimate variant, that variant is named as a threat and handled in Section 6.

## 3.1 Guerrilla Marketing

Guerrilla marketing applies the logic of asymmetric irregular warfare: high-impact, low-resource actions that surprise, reframe, or embarrass larger competitors. Examples include ambush campaigns, experiential activations that generate organic narrative momentum, and rapid exploitation of competitor missteps. Success depends on speed, local knowledge of audience psychology, and the ability to withdraw or pivot before costly counter-action.

**Doctrinal logic.** The term "guerrilla marketing" was popularized by Jay Conrad Levinson's 1984 book of that name, written for small businesses that could not outspend larger rivals (Levinson 1984). The parallel with irregular warfare is direct: concentrate scarce resources at the point and moment of greatest effect, rely on surprise and local knowledge, and avoid set-piece battles against superior budgets.

**Corporate applications.**

- **Category reframing.** Rather than compete on the incumbent's terms, a challenger names a new problem or a new standard and shows evidence that it matters. A security vendor might publish a free, reproducible assessment method for a risk the market under-weights. The method becomes the frame in which buyers then compare products.
- **Experiential activations.** Physical or digital experiences that people choose to share: a live demonstration at an industry event, a public challenge with transparent rules, a pop-up that solves a real problem. Activations should have the venue's and the city's permission, honor event rules, and avoid implying sponsorship that does not exist.
- **Newsjacking with substance.** When a relevant news event breaks, the fastest credible expert voice often defines the conversation. That means pre-approved spokespeople, a library of verified facts, and a rule that commentary adds information rather than exploiting tragedy.
- **Comparative challenger messaging.** Truthful, substantiated comparisons with named competitors are lawful in the United States and are encouraged by the FTC's policy statement on comparative advertising, provided they are clear and not deceptive (FTC 1979). Claims must be substantiated before publication, and comparisons must be like for like.
- **Rapid exploitation of competitor missteps—carefully.** When a competitor suffers an outage, price rise or policy change, a challenger can lawfully offer a migration path, a guarantee or an honest comparison. It must not spread rumors, exaggerate the competitor's problem or imply facts it cannot prove.
- **Community-first launches.** Seed a product with a small, highly engaged community, such as practitioners, user groups or open-source contributors, whose genuine enthusiasm carries further than paid reach.

**Boundaries.** Ambush tactics around major events can collide with trademark law and event-specific advertising restrictions. False or misleading claims about one's own or a competitor's products create liability under Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1125) and under Section 5 of the FTC Act, as explained in the FTC's Policy Statement on Deception (FTC 1983). Stunts that put the public at risk, trespass or deceive journalists destroy the legitimacy that guerrilla marketing depends on.

**Measures.** Share of voice in the target segment; earned-media mentions that carry the intended frame; inbound inquiries attributable to the activation; shift in the criteria buyers cite in sales conversations.

## 3.2 Marketing Psychological Operations (Persuasion Psychology)

Marketing psyops design messages to exploit cognitive biases, emotional triggers, and pre-existing belief structures. Techniques include fear-of-missing-out framing, social proof cascades, identity-based appeals, and controlled ambiguity. Unlike crude propaganda, effective corporate psyops maintain plausible alignment with brand values while systematically shifting perception thresholds.

In this expanded edition, "marketing psyops" means the disciplined, ethical use of persuasion psychology. The word "exploit" in the original description should be read in its strategic sense: to make full use of how people actually decide. It does not mean taking advantage of people against their interests. That distinction is the line between persuasion and manipulation, and it is the subject of active regulation.

**Doctrinal logic.** Military psychological operations aim to influence the emotions, motives and reasoning of selected audiences. The commercial counterpart rests on a large body of research on judgment and decision-making.

- **Framing.** Tversky and Kahneman showed that logically equivalent descriptions of a choice can produce different preferences depending on how outcomes are framed (Tversky and Kahneman 1981). Prospect theory shows that people weigh losses more heavily than equivalent gains (Kahneman and Tversky 1979).
- **Principles of influence.** Robert Cialdini's work describes recurring levers of persuasion—reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity and, in the 2021 edition, unity (Cialdini 2021).
- **Psychological targeting.** Matz and colleagues showed in field experiments that tailoring persuasive appeals to people's psychological characteristics, inferred from digital footprints, changed their behavior. The authors warned that the same method "could be used to covertly exploit weaknesses in their character and persuade them to take action against their own best interest, highlighting the potential need for policy interventions" (Matz et al. 2017).

**Corporate applications.**

- **Loss framing of real risks.** A cybersecurity firm can truthfully frame the cost of inaction—downtime, regulatory exposure, recovery effort—using published data, rather than only describing features. Loss framing is legitimate when the loss is real and quantified honestly.
- **Genuine social proof.** Verified customer counts, named references with permission, independent reviews and case studies. Social proof is persuasive because people assume it is real, which is why fabricating it is unlawful (16 CFR Part 465).
- **Genuine scarcity and urgency.** Limited seats at a workshop, a real deadline for a price, an actual production run. False countdown timers and fake "only two left" messages are among the practices the FTC staff has identified as dark patterns (FTC 2022).
- **Authority through evidence.** Publishing methods, data and peer-reviewed or independently tested results, and letting credentialed experts speak in their own names.
- **Identity and unity appeals.** Speaking to the professional identity of an audience ("for the people who keep hospitals running"), communities of practice and shared values, without stereotyping or exclusion.
- **Commitment ladders.** Small, honest first steps—a free assessment, a pilot, a checklist—that let buyers test a claim before committing more. The buyer reaches the conclusion through their own experience, which is the ethical version of the "voluntary" decision at the heart of reflexive control.
- **Strategic ambiguity, properly bounded.** The original paper's "controlled ambiguity" is legitimate when it means leaving room for audiences to project their own aspirations onto a brand ("built for what's next"). It is illegitimate when vague language creates a false impression about price, performance or terms. Material terms must be clear.

**The persuasion–manipulation line.** A practical test for every persuasive technique: *Would the audience still make this choice if they fully understood what we did and why?* If yes, it is persuasion. If the technique only works because the audience does not notice it—hidden fees, pre-checked boxes, confirm-shaming, disguised ads, obstruction of cancellation—it is manipulation, and regulators treat it as such (FTC 2022; FTC 1983).

**Measures.** Message-testing lift in comprehension and preference; change in stated decision criteria; complaint and refund rates as a guardrail metric (rising complaints signal persuasion tipping into manipulation).

## 3.3 Algorithmic Optimization ("Algorithmic Manipulation")

Modern platforms optimize for engagement. Corporate Cognitive Warfare treats ranking, recommendation, and discovery algorithms as contested terrain. The original edition of this paper used the term "algorithmic manipulation" for this terrain. In this expanded edition, the legitimate practice is described as **algorithmic optimization**: making truthful, useful content the path of least cognitive resistance by understanding how ranking and recommendation systems work and meeting their quality signals honestly. The objective is to make desired narratives the path of least cognitive resistance.

The original description listed tactics including "synthetic engagement networks (within platform rules)" and "the deliberate shaping of training data or trend signals." This edition narrows both. Synthetic engagement—fake accounts, purchased likes or views, automated networks posing as people—is prohibited by the major platforms and, where it creates fake indicators of social media influence, by the FTC's 2024 rule (X Corp. n.d.; YouTube n.d.; Meta n.d.; 16 CFR § 465.8). It is therefore treated in Section 6 as a threat. "Shaping training data" is legitimate only in the sense of publishing accurate, well-sourced, crawlable information about one's own organization. Planting false or misleading content to influence AI systems is a form of the data manipulation that the defense literature warns about (Pollpeter and Kerrigan 2021).

**Doctrinal logic.** Militaries treat the electromagnetic spectrum and information networks as terrain. For a company, ranking systems are terrain in the same sense: they determine what audiences see. The lawful approach is to understand the terrain, not to corrupt it.

**Corporate applications.**

- **People-first search content.** Google's guidance on "helpful, reliable, people-first content" and its spam policies describe what ranks and what is penalized (Google n.d.-a; Google n.d.-b). The spam policies define practices such as scaled content abuse ("many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users"), link spam (including "buying or selling links for ranking purposes") and cloaking. The lawful strategy is original research, expert authorship, clear structure, technical health and earned links.
- **Generative engine optimization (GEO).** AI assistants that synthesize answers from web sources are a new discovery layer. Aggarwal and colleagues introduced GEO as a framework for content creators and reported that GEO methods can "boost visibility by up to 40%" in generative engine responses, with effectiveness varying by domain (Aggarwal et al. 2024). The lawful takeaways are to publish clear, citable, well-sourced material: definitions, statistics with sources, quotations from named experts, and structured facts about the organization that an AI system can retrieve and attribute accurately.
- **Platform-native formats and cadence.** Each platform rewards different formats: short video, documents, threads, live sessions. Publishing in native formats, at times when the real audience is active, with accessible captions and alt text, is optimization without deception.
- **Authentic engagement design.** Content that invites substantive replies (questions, polls with real stakes, practitioner debates) earns genuine engagement signals. Engagement bait—content that exists only to solicit likes or shares—runs against platform policies (YouTube n.d.).
- **Owned discovery assets.** Newsletters, podcasts, communities and documentation that do not depend on any single algorithm. Irregular forces keep their own lines of communication.
- **Structured data and entity hygiene.** Consistent names, descriptions, leadership facts and contact details across the web reduce the chance that search engines and AI systems misstate who the organization is.

**A caution from the research.** In a study of rumor cascades on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, Vosoughi, Roy and Aral found that false news reached more people than the truth and spread faster, and suggested that novelty and the emotional reactions of recipients may help explain the difference (Vosoughi et al. 2018). Engagement-optimized systems can reward sensationalism. A company that chases engagement with exaggeration may win the moment and lose its credibility. The disciplined approach is to make true content as novel, clear and emotionally resonant as possible.

**Measures.** Organic visibility for priority topics; share of citations in AI-generated answers for category questions; engagement quality (saves, replies, shares by relevant accounts) rather than raw counts; accuracy of AI-generated descriptions of the company.

## 3.4 Social Influence Operations

Social influence campaigns identify and activate key nodes—creators, employees, analysts, community leaders—to propagate narratives organically. Methods include relationship mapping, incentive alignment, sharing of talking points, and the creation of self-reinforcing social proof. Authentic micro-influencers often outperform paid mega-endorsements because they carry higher perceived legitimacy.

**Doctrinal logic.** Irregular warfare is population-centric, and populations are networks. Granovetter's classic work on "the strength of weak ties" showed that acquaintances, not close friends, are often the bridges through which new information travels between groups (Granovetter 1973). Influence strategy therefore cares about bridges: the people who connect communities.

**Corporate applications.**

- **Relationship mapping.** Identify, from public information, the voices a target audience trusts: practitioners who write, conference speakers, community moderators, analysts, journalists. Map who bridges which communities. Respect privacy law when building such maps and use them to prioritize outreach, not to profile individuals invasively.
- **Creator partnerships with disclosure.** Paid or gifted creator relationships are lawful and effective when the material connection is disclosed clearly. The FTC's Endorsement Guides were revised in 2023 (16 CFR Part 255), and its *Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers* explains that disclosures must be clear and placed with the endorsement, not buried in a profile, in a pile of hashtags or behind a "more" link (FTC n.d.). Contracts should require disclosure, prohibit claims the brand cannot substantiate and allow the brand to monitor compliance.
- **Employee advocacy.** Employees are credible messengers. Programs that give staff accurate content to share are lawful when employees share voluntarily and identify their employment where they endorse the company's products. The Endorsement Guides treat employees' connections as material connections that should be disclosed (16 CFR Part 255). Never script employees to pose as independent customers.
- **Analyst and expert relations.** Brief analysts with evidence, provide reference customers, and accept that independent experts will criticize. Paying for "independent" commentary without disclosure is deceptive.
- **Customer communities.** User groups, forums and councils where customers help each other build durable social proof. Moderate fairly, and never suppress negative reviews. Review suppression is a violation of the FTC's 2024 reviews rule (16 CFR § 465.7).
- **Talking points that equip rather than script.** Give advocates accurate facts, data and stories, then let them speak in their own words. Identical copy-pasted messages across many accounts look—and can be—coordinated inauthentic behavior.
- **Coalitions with transparent membership.** Industry associations and coalitions can lawfully advocate for shared positions, provided membership and funding are disclosed as the law requires. A coalition that hides its sponsors is astroturfing (Section 6).

**Measures.** Reach and engagement among priority communities; proportion of mentions coming from independent voices; sentiment among bridging nodes; disclosure compliance rate across partner content (target: 100 percent).

## 3.5 Targeted Paid Advertising

Precision advertising supplies the scalable delivery mechanism for cognitive effects. Behavioral and contextual targeting, sequential messaging, and dynamic creative optimization allow operators to deliver tailored narratives to specific decision-makers or audience segments. When integrated with organic and algorithmic efforts, paid media accelerates and protects narrative momentum.

The original description included psychographic targeting. That remains possible, but it is the area of targeting most constrained by law and most open to abuse (Matz et al. 2017). This edition treats it with corresponding care.

**Corporate applications.**

- **Account-based marketing (ABM).** In business-to-business markets, ads and content aimed at the members of a specific buying committee—finance, technical, executive—each addressing that role's real concerns with evidence.
- **Sequential messaging.** A planned narrative sequence: first the problem, then the evidence, then the proof, then the offer. Each step is truthful and stands on its own.
- **Contextual targeting.** Placing ads beside relevant content rather than relying on personal data. Contextual approaches reduce privacy risk and are increasingly effective as tracking declines.
- **First-party data with consent.** Audiences built from people who have chosen to engage (subscribers, event attendees, customers), with a clear lawful basis and honored opt-outs.
- **Dynamic creative optimization with guardrails.** Automated testing of headlines and visuals, constrained to pre-approved, substantiated claims so the optimizer cannot "discover" a misleading message that happens to perform well.
- **Defensive paid media.** Buying visibility on one's own brand terms and crisis-related queries so that accurate information appears where people look, especially during an incident.
- **Recruitment advertising.** Targeted employer-brand campaigns, subject to anti-discrimination law in employment advertising and to platform rules for employment ads.

**Boundaries.** In the EU, the GDPR requires a lawful basis for processing personal data, gives people an absolute right to object to direct marketing, and restricts processing of special categories of data such as health, religion or political opinions (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). The Digital Services Act requires online platforms to show users that content is an advertisement, on whose behalf it is presented, and the main parameters used to target it. It prohibits ads based on profiling with special categories of personal data and profiling-based ads to users the platform knows with reasonable certainty are minors (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Articles 26 and 28). In California, the CCPA as amended by the CPRA gives consumers the right to opt out of the sale or sharing of their personal information, including through the Global Privacy Control (California DOJ n.d.). Platform advertising policies add further limits on sensitive targeting and categories. The practical rule: if a targeting choice would embarrass the company when explained to the person targeted, do not make it.

**Measures.** Incremental lift (measured with holdout tests, not attribution alone); cost per qualified opportunity; brand-search lift; opt-out and complaint rates as guardrails.

## 3.6 Additional Irregular Warfare Adaptations

- Intelligence preparation of the battlespace: systematic mapping of audience beliefs, competitor positioning, and information pathways, using lawful, public and consented sources.
- Disclosure discipline and counter-deception: deciding deliberately what to disclose and when, within securities, consumer and employment law, and protecting plans from leaks. The original edition listed "deception and misdirection." In lawful practice, a company may keep its plans confidential and choose the timing of truthful announcements. It may not make false statements, plant fake "leaks" or mislead markets. Detecting deception aimed at the company is a core defensive task (Section 6).
- Rapid adaptation cycles: continuous sensing and response loops that treat market feedback as real-time intelligence.
- Narrative defense and resilience: internal inoculation, monitoring of emerging threats, and pre-positioned counter-narratives built on verified facts.

## 3.7 Integrating the Five Techniques

The techniques are most effective as a combined system rather than as separate tools. The irregular-warfare analogy is combined arms: each element covers another's weakness.

- **Guerrilla actions create the spark.** They are cheap, surprising and newsworthy, but they fade quickly without follow-through.
- **Persuasion psychology shapes the message** so that the spark carries a clear, truthful frame instead of a forgettable stunt.
- **Algorithmic optimization extends reach and lifespan**, so that people searching or asking an AI assistant later find the same frame and the evidence behind it.
- **Social influence supplies credibility.** Independent and disclosed voices repeat the frame in their own words to the communities they bridge.
- **Targeted advertising adds precision and protection.** It puts the sequence in front of the specific decision-makers who matter, and holds the space on key queries during a crisis.

A simple integration test for any campaign plan: for each audience in the Understand map, can the team name the spark, the frame, the place the audience will find it again, the trusted voice who will confirm it, and the paid support that will reach the people who matter most? Gaps in that chain are where campaigns usually fail, and they are also where a competitor or hostile actor is most likely to move in.
