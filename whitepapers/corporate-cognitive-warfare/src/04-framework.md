# 4. Operational Framework

A practical Corporate Cognitive Warfare campaign follows a six-phase cycle:

1. Understand: map cognitive terrain, key audiences, belief systems, and information pathways.
2. Shape: design narratives and messages that use existing cognitive tendencies truthfully while remaining brand-consistent.
3. Seed: introduce content and talking points through organic, algorithmic, and paid channels, with every paid or material relationship disclosed.
4. Amplify: activate genuine social proof, disclosed advocates, and authentic algorithmic signals to achieve critical mass.
5. Protect: monitor for counter-narratives and adversary responses; adjust or inoculate.
6. Assess: measure changes in perception, decision criteria, and behavior; refine for the next cycle.

The cycle mirrors the military planning loop of intelligence, planning, execution and assessment. What makes it a *corporate* doctrine is that each phase passes through a gate: a short, explicit check that the work is truthful, disclosed, lawful and consistent with platform rules before it moves forward.

## 4.1 Phase Detail

**Understand.** Build an "intelligence preparation of the cognitive terrain." Inputs include customer interviews, win/loss analysis, search and social listening, analyst reports, review-platform content, AI-assistant answers about the category, and competitors' public statements. Outputs are an audience map (who decides, who influences, who bridges communities), a belief map (what each audience currently believes and why), a pathway map (where each audience gets information), and a threat map (who might contest the organization's narrative, and how). The reflexive-control lens adds a key question: how do the audience and the competitors model *us*?

**Shape.** Translate intelligence into a narrative architecture: one core proposition, three to five supporting claims, and the evidence for each. Each claim is checked for substantiation before it is used. Messages are tested for comprehension and persuasion with real members of the audience. The persuasion–manipulation test from Section 3.2 applies to every creative concept.

**Seed.** Introduce the narrative where the audience already is. Owned channels carry the full evidence. Earned channels—press, analysts, creators—carry independent validation. Paid channels add reach and sequence. Disclosures, consent and platform rules are built into the briefs, not added afterwards.

**Amplify.** Concentrate effort where momentum is forming. Bring in disclosed advocates, invite customers to share real experiences, answer questions in public, and put paid support behind content that is already performing organically. Amplification never includes fake accounts, purchased engagement or coordinated posting that misrepresents independence.

**Protect.** Monitor for hostile or mistaken narratives, impersonation, fake reviews and coordinated inauthentic activity. Prebunk known lines of attack, keep a verified fact base ready, and set response thresholds in advance (Section 6). Protect the organization's own decision-making from provocation.

**Assess.** Measure what changed in belief and behavior, not just in activity. Feed the lessons into the next cycle, retire claims that did not land, and record what the competition did in response.

## 4.2 Phase Gates and Outputs

| Phase | Key outputs | Gate question | Example measures |
| Understand | Audience, belief, pathway and threat maps | Was the intelligence gathered lawfully, from public or consented sources? | Coverage of priority audiences; freshness of data |
| Shape | Narrative architecture; tested messages | Is every claim true, substantiated and clear on material terms? | Comprehension and preference lift in testing |
| Seed | Channel plan; creator and partner briefs | Are all material connections disclosed and all targeting choices lawful? | Placement quality; disclosure compliance rate |
| Amplify | Advocate activation; paid support | Is all engagement authentic and every account real? | Share of voice; independent mentions |
| Protect | Monitoring; prebunks; response playbook | Are responses accurate, proportionate and pre-authorized? | Time to detect; time to verified response |
| Assess | Results review; lessons; next-cycle plan | Did we change beliefs without eroding trust? | Decision-criteria shift; trust and complaint metrics |

## 4.3 Metrics That Matter

Cognitive campaigns fail when they measure activity instead of effect. A useful metric stack has four layers:

- **Exposure:** reach, impressions, share of voice, share of AI-answer citations. Necessary, but never sufficient.
- **Perception:** awareness of the core proposition, association of the brand with the chosen frame, trust and credibility scores, sentiment among bridging voices.
- **Decision:** change in the criteria buyers use, inclusion in shortlists, win rates in contested deals, candidate acceptance rates, investor questions that reflect the intended narrative.
- **Integrity:** complaint rates, refund rates, regulatory inquiries, platform enforcement actions, disclosure compliance, and the accuracy of third-party descriptions of the company. Integrity metrics are guardrails: if they worsen while the others improve, the campaign is borrowing against trust.

## 4.4 Tempo

Irregular warfare rewards tempo, the ability to observe, decide and act faster than the opponent. In corporate terms, tempo comes from pre-approval. Spokespeople, fact bases, claim libraries, crisis holding statements and creator guidelines approved in advance let the team move in hours rather than weeks, without skipping the gates. Speed and compliance are not in tension when the compliance work is done before the race starts.
