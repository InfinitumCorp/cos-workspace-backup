# 5. Use Cases

The twelve use cases below show the doctrine at work. They are illustrative composites, not accounts of real clients, campaigns or results. Each follows the same pattern: the situation, the objective, the approach, the guardrails and the measures of success.

## Use Case 1: The Challenger Reframes the Category

**Situation.** A mid-size software company competes with an incumbent whose brand dominates analyst reports and buyer shortlists. Head-to-head feature comparisons favor the incumbent's breadth.

**Objective.** Change the criteria buyers use, so that the challenger's real strength—time to value—becomes a deciding factor.

**Approach.** In the Understand phase, win/loss interviews show that buyers repeatedly complain about long implementations but never put implementation time in their requirements. The challenger publishes an open, reproducible method for measuring time to value, invites customers and competitors' customers to benchmark themselves, and releases anonymized aggregate results with its methodology. Sales teams use the method in discovery conversations. Analysts are briefed with the data. A sequential paid campaign aimed at operations leaders leads with the cost of delay, then the method, then customer evidence.

**Reflexive lens.** The incumbent's likely response is to dismiss the metric. The challenger anticipates this by making the method open and inviting independent replication, so a dismissal looks defensive.

**Guardrails.** All benchmark data is real, consented and methodologically documented. Comparative claims are substantiated before publication (FTC 1979).

**Measures.** Proportion of requests for proposals that include time-to-value criteria; shortlist inclusion; analyst mentions of the metric.

## Use Case 2: A Guerrilla Launch in a Crowded Consumer Market

**Situation.** A small consumer brand launches a durable product into a market saturated with heavily advertised alternatives.

**Objective.** Reach early adopters and earn credible word of mouth without a large media budget.

**Approach.** The brand runs a permitted public "durability challenge" at a busy venue, with transparent rules and live results. It seeds the product with micro-creators in relevant hobbyist communities, all of whom are contracted to disclose the relationship clearly. It opens a community forum where buyers can post genuine reviews, positive or negative.

**Guardrails.** Creator contracts require clear "#ad" or "sponsored" disclosures placed with the endorsement (FTC n.d.). No incentive is tied to positive sentiment, and no reviews are suppressed (16 CFR §§ 465.4, 465.7). The activation has venue and municipal permission.

**Measures.** Earned mentions; organic search growth for the brand name; review volume and rating distribution; repeat purchase rate.

## Use Case 3: Countering a Viral Falsehood About Product Safety

**Situation.** A manipulated video falsely suggesting a safety defect in a company's product spreads rapidly on social platforms.

**Objective.** Contain the falsehood, protect customers' trust, and contest the "right to define the event."

**Approach.** The Protect phase was prepared in advance: a monitoring service flags the spike within the hour, and a pre-approved response protocol is triggered. The company publishes a clear, evidence-based statement with test data and an independent laboratory's confirmation, reports the manipulated media to each platform under its policies, briefs journalists with verifiable facts, and answers customer questions directly. Following correction research, the response leads with the fact, explains why the claim is wrong, and avoids repeating the false claim more than necessary (Lewandowsky et al. 2012).

**Guardrails.** The company does not speculate about who made the video without evidence. Any legal action proceeds through counsel. Real safety concerns raised during the episode are investigated, not dismissed.

**Measures.** Time to detection; time to verified response; share of conversation carrying the correction; customer-service contact volume returning to baseline.

## Use Case 4: Defending Against a Fake-Review Attack

**Situation.** A regional services firm sees a sudden cluster of one-star reviews from accounts with no history, posted within hours and using similar wording.

**Objective.** Restore an accurate picture of customer experience and deter repetition.

**Approach.** The firm documents the pattern (timing, account age, text similarity), reports it to the review platforms with evidence, and responds publicly and politely to each review, inviting the reviewer to make contact with order details. It strengthens its genuine review pipeline by asking every real customer for honest feedback after service, without incentives tied to sentiment.

**Guardrails.** The firm does not retaliate with fake positive reviews, does not threaten reviewers, and does not suppress legitimate negative reviews—each of which would breach the FTC's rule (16 CFR Part 465).

**Measures.** Removal rate of reported reviews; trend in verified review volume; conversion rate from review-platform visits.

## Use Case 5: Winning the Talent Contest

**Situation.** A technology firm competes for scarce security engineers against larger employers with bigger brands.

**Objective.** Make the firm the preferred choice for a specific, high-value candidate segment.

**Approach.** Intelligence work shows that target candidates value interesting problems, learning time and flexible work more than headline salary. The firm's engineers publish technical write-ups under their own names, speak at practitioner conferences and run a public capture-the-flag event. An employee advocacy program offers optional, accurate content for staff to share.

**Guardrails.** Employees who post about open roles identify their employment. Participation is voluntary. Job ads comply with anti-discrimination rules, and targeting avoids protected characteristics.

**Measures.** Applications from the target segment; offer acceptance rate; time to hire; employee retention.

## Use Case 6: The Investor Narrative and Disclosure Discipline

**Situation.** A listed company plans a strategic shift that will depress near-term margins.

**Objective.** Help investors understand the strategy and its timeline so that the share price reflects long-term value rather than short-term surprise.

**Approach.** The company prepares a coherent narrative—why the shift, what milestones will show progress, and what risks remain—and discloses it publicly and simultaneously to all investors, consistent with Regulation FD (17 CFR Part 243). Management commits to reporting the same milestones every quarter, so the company keeps the right to "judge the outcome" credibly.

**Guardrails.** No selective disclosure of material nonpublic information, no misleading projections, and no "leaks" designed to move the price. Investor relations, legal and finance approve all materials.

**Measures.** Analyst models reflecting the stated milestones; volatility around announcements; quality of investor questions.

## Use Case 7: Lawful Engagement in a Policy Debate

**Situation.** A proposed regulation would affect an industry in which a company operates.

**Objective.** Ensure that regulators see accurate evidence about the regulation's effects.

**Approach.** The company submits public comments with data and methodology, joins an industry association whose membership is public, sponsors independent research with disclosed funding and no control over conclusions, and registers lobbying activity where the law requires it.

**Guardrails.** No fabricated grassroots campaigns, no form letters submitted under invented names, and no undisclosed funding of "independent" voices. Each of these is astroturfing (Section 6).

**Measures.** Inclusion of the company's evidence in the regulatory record; quality of the final rule from the company's perspective; reputation with regulators.

## Use Case 8: Account-Based Influence on a Buying Committee

**Situation.** An enterprise-services firm pursues fifty named target accounts with multi-person buying committees.

**Objective.** Shift each committee member's beliefs about the problem and the firm's credibility before a formal procurement begins.

**Approach.** For each role, the firm identifies the decisive fear and the evidence that addresses it: total-cost models for finance, technical proofs for engineering, peer references for executives. It runs role-specific sequential advertising, offers working sessions and invites committee members to private peer roundtables with existing customers.

**Guardrails.** Targeting uses business contact data with a lawful basis and honored opt-outs under the GDPR and the CCPA. No sensitive categories are used. All claims are substantiated.

**Measures.** Engagement by role within target accounts; meetings set; inclusion in requests for proposals; win rate.

## Use Case 9: Market Entry Into a New Country

**Situation.** A firm enters a market where it is unknown and where local competitors enjoy deep trust.

**Objective.** Earn legitimacy quickly.

**Approach.** The Understand phase studies local information habits, trusted institutions, regulatory expectations and cultural references. The firm hires local leaders who speak for it in their own names, partners with respected local institutions, localizes rather than translates its content, and makes visible commitments such as local support, data residency where relevant and community investment.

**Guardrails.** Full compliance with local advertising, privacy and consumer law; no claims about competitors that cannot be substantiated under local rules.

**Measures.** Aided awareness; trust measures relative to local competitors; partner and channel recruitment.

## Use Case 10: Executive Impersonation and Deepfakes

**Situation.** Fraudsters use a cloned voice of the chief financial officer to request an urgent payment, and a fake executive social-media account begins posting market-moving statements.

**Objective.** Prevent fraud, correct the record and protect market integrity.

**Approach.** The company's resilience plan already requires out-of-band verification for payment requests and a shared verification phrase for executives. Finance staff follow the procedure and the fraud fails. The communications team reports the impersonating account under platform policies, publishes the list of official accounts, and issues a statement through verified channels. Over time the company adopts content-provenance practices for official media, such as the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) specifications (C2PA n.d.), and monitors for AI-generated content subject to labeling duties, such as those in Article 50 of the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).

**Guardrails.** Statements identify facts, not suspects, unless law enforcement confirms them.

**Measures.** Fraud attempts blocked; time to takedown; accuracy of media coverage.

## Use Case 11: Visibility in AI-Generated Answers

**Situation.** A professional-services firm finds that AI assistants describe its category without mentioning it, and sometimes misdescribe its services.

**Objective.** Ensure accurate, attributable representation in generative-engine answers.

**Approach.** The firm publishes well-structured explainers with clear definitions, sourced statistics and quotations from named experts; corrects inconsistent facts about itself across directories and profiles; and publishes original research that others cite. These steps reflect both people-first search guidance and GEO research on citability (Google n.d.-a; Aggarwal et al. 2024).

**Guardrails.** No mass-produced low-value pages (scaled content abuse), no purchased links, and no false claims planted to influence AI systems (Google n.d.-b).

**Measures.** Share of AI answers that cite or mention the firm for priority questions; accuracy of descriptions; referral traffic from AI surfaces.

## Use Case 12: The Internal Narrative During a Merger

**Situation.** After an acquisition, rumors about layoffs and culture clashes spread among employees of both companies, and competitors' recruiters circulate them.

**Objective.** Retain key people and maintain performance through integration.

**Approach.** Leadership treats employees as a priority audience. It publishes a clear integration timeline, commits to dates for decisions, holds open question sessions, equips managers with accurate answers, and sets up a channel where rumors can be raised and checked. Prebunking explains in advance the kinds of rumors that typically circulate during mergers and how to verify them (Roozenbeek et al. 2022).

**Guardrails.** Leaders do not promise what they cannot guarantee. Employment-law and works-council obligations are honored.

**Measures.** Regretted attrition; engagement-survey trust scores; rumor volume in the verification channel.
