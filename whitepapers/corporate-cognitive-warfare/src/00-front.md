# About This Edition

This is the expanded edition of the white paper *Corporate Cognitive Warfare: Irregular Warfare for Market Supremacy*, written by William Aaron Turner, Founder and President of Infinitum Corporation, who coined the term "Corporate Cognitive Warfare" in 2026. The original approved paper set out the doctrine in brief. This edition keeps its structure, its definitions and its six-phase framework. It adds four bodies of material: a doctrinal foundation drawn from published military literature, detailed corporate applications for each technique, an extended set of illustrative use cases, and a dedicated section on defense and counter-influence.

**Neutrality statement.** The military material in Section 2 presents doctrinal approaches analytically. It describes what published sources say about how different military traditions think about perception, decision-making and influence. It takes no side and makes no claim about any party, and it does not praise, blame or score any state, army, government or leader. Where the analytic literature on a concept comes mainly from outside the tradition that produced it, the paper says so. Accounts of particular campaigns or attributions of specific operations are deliberately left out. The paper is about concepts and their lessons for organizations, not about conflicts.

**Ethics statement.** Every corporate application in this paper is meant to be used within the law and within the rules of the platforms involved. That means truthful advertising, clear disclosure of paid and material relationships, respect for privacy law, and authentic accounts and engagement. Deceptive tactics such as fake accounts, bot networks, astroturfing, fabricated reviews and disinformation are described only as threats to recognize and defend against. The paper gives no instructions for using them.

**Illustrative material.** The use cases in Section 5 are composites written to show how the doctrine applies. They do not describe real clients, real campaigns or results achieved by Infinitum Corporation.

**Sources.** Every source cited here is public and was checked before release: journal articles by DOI, and reports, statutes and policies at their official URLs. Quotations are reproduced as they appear in the cited source. The full list is in the References section.

# Executive Summary

Corporate Cognitive Warfare is the deliberate application of irregular warfare principles—guerrilla tactics, marketing psychology, influence campaigns, algorithmic discovery, and asymmetric information dominance—to the corporate competitive environment. Coined by William Aaron Turner, Founder and President of Infinitum Corporation, the term describes a strategic paradigm in which organizations treat the cognitive space of customers, employees, investors, competitors, and regulators as a contested battlespace.

Unlike conventional marketing, Corporate Cognitive Warfare seeks not merely to promote products but to shape how target audiences perceive reality, make decisions, and allocate attention and resources. It operates below the threshold of open conflict, using adaptive, low-signature methods to achieve durable cognitive advantage. Throughout this paper, "low-signature" means quiet, efficient and well-targeted. It never means hidden from the audience in ways the law or platform rules require to be disclosed.

This white paper defines the doctrine, maps irregular warfare techniques onto corporate applications, and provides a practical framework for both offensive employment and defensive resilience. It emphasizes ethical boundaries, legal compliance, and the long-term strategic value of trust preservation.

The expanded edition develops that argument in five ways.

First, it grounds the concept in military doctrine. Militaries in several traditions concluded long ago that perception and decision-making are decisive terrain. Soviet and Russian writers developed *reflexive control*, the theory of conveying "specially prepared information" to incline an opponent "to voluntarily make the predetermined decision" (Chotikul 1986). Chinese military writers have developed the "three warfares" of public opinion, psychological and legal warfare, and more recently *cognitive domain operations*, a concept that analysts describe as seeking "mind superiority" (Kania 2016; Beauchamp-Mustafaga 2019). NATO's scientific community now treats cognitive warfare as an area of study in its own right and stresses resilience as a primary response (NATO STO n.d.). These traditions differ in vocabulary and purpose. All of them treat the human mind as terrain. Section 2 presents them neutrally, from published sources, and draws out lessons that transfer to commercial competition.

Second, it gives detailed, lawful corporate applications for each of the five core techniques: guerrilla marketing, marketing psychology (ethical persuasion), algorithmic optimization (legitimate SEO/GEO), social influence and targeted advertising. Each application comes with the legal and platform rules that bound it, including the U.S. Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides and its 2024 rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Digital Services Act (DSA), and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA).

Third, it sets out twelve illustrative use cases. They range from a challenger reframing a category to a firm defending itself against a fake-review attack or an executive deepfake.

Fourth, it adds a defensive doctrine. The same techniques that win attention can be turned against an organization by competitors, activists, fraudsters or opportunists, and the illegitimate versions—coordinated inauthentic behavior, bot networks, fabricated reviews, impersonation and disinformation—are now routine risks. Section 6 shows how to detect, withstand and answer them, drawing on research on psychological inoculation and misinformation correction.

Fifth, it turns the doctrine into an operating model: roles, gates, metrics, a 90-day start-up plan and a maturity path.

Key takeaways:

- The decisive terrain in modern markets is cognitive. Attention, trust and the framing of choices often decide outcomes before price or features are compared.
- Military doctrine offers a disciplined vocabulary for that terrain: modeling how the other side reasons, shaping the frame in which decisions are made, contesting narratives, and building resilience against hostile influence.
- In commerce, the advantage is durable only when it is earned with truthful claims, disclosed relationships, authentic engagement and respect for privacy. Deception is a strategic liability as well as a legal one.
- Every organization is already a target. A cognitive defense capability—monitoring, prebunking, rapid and accurate response, and decision hygiene—is now basic organizational hygiene.
- Smaller organizations can win. Asymmetry favors the side that understands the audience best and moves fastest, not the side that spends most.

# 1. Defining Corporate Cognitive Warfare

Corporate Cognitive Warfare is irregular warfare conducted in the commercial domain. It is the systematic effort to gain, maintain, and protect cognitive superiority over relevant audiences in order to advance organizational objectives—market share, valuation, talent acquisition, regulatory outcomes, or competitive displacement.

## 1.1 Origin of the Term

William Aaron Turner coined the term "Corporate Cognitive Warfare" in 2026 to name a reality he saw in modern markets and in the security work Infinitum Corporation does. Organizations already compete through attention, belief and decision-making, yet most of them manage those contests piecemeal: brand in one department, communications in another, competitive intelligence in a third, and trust and safety, if it exists, somewhere else. The term pulls these activities into a single discipline with a single question: what do the people who matter to us believe, why do they believe it, and how is that belief being contested?

The word "warfare" is used deliberately and analytically. It signals that the cognitive environment is contested, that other actors are working to shape the same audiences, and that some of them do not follow the rules. It does not license treating customers as enemies, and it does not license deception. The military vocabulary is a lens for disciplined thinking about competition, in the same way that strategy, campaigns, intelligence and logistics long ago became ordinary management language.

## 1.2 Key Characteristics

Key characteristics include:

- Asymmetry: smaller or agile actors can challenge larger incumbents by targeting decision-making processes rather than resources.
- Ambiguity of signature: much cognitive competition looks like normal business activity—a well-timed article, a product demonstration, a talk at an industry event—so its strategic intent is not obvious to observers. In lawful practice, that ambiguity concerns strategy only. It never extends to hiding who is speaking or who paid.
- Cognitive focus: success is measured by changed beliefs, priorities, and behaviors, not just awareness or clicks.
- Continuum of competition: activity persists across peacetime marketing, crisis response, and contested markets.
- Multi-audience terrain: customers, employees, investors, partners, regulators, journalists and analysts all hold beliefs that matter, and what is said to one audience reaches the others.
- Machine-mediated reach: most messages now arrive through ranking and recommendation systems, and increasingly through generative AI answers, so understanding those systems is part of understanding the terrain.

## 1.3 What Corporate Cognitive Warfare Is Not

Because the vocabulary is martial, it is worth being precise about the boundaries.

It is not disinformation. Disinformation is false information knowingly shared to cause harm (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017). A company that spreads falsehoods about a competitor exposes itself to false-advertising liability and to the collapse of the trust it was trying to win.

It is not coordinated inauthentic behavior. Major platforms define and remove networks that use fake identities to mislead people about who is behind them (Meta n.d.; X Corp. n.d.). Such networks are threats to defend against, not tools.

It is not covert. Commercial influence must be identifiable as such where the law requires it. Paid endorsements carry disclosures, sponsored content is labeled, and executives speak in their own names.

It is not exploitation of the vulnerable. Targeting children, people in crisis or people with sensitive characteristics with manipulative techniques is unethical and, in many jurisdictions, unlawful.

What it is: a structured way to understand the cognitive terrain, frame choices truthfully and compellingly, win attention through legitimate means, build credible advocates, and defend the organization's reputation and decision-making against hostile influence.

## 1.4 The Cognitive Terrain: Five Audiences

The original paper names customers, employees, investors, competitors and regulators. Each is a distinct piece of terrain with its own beliefs, information pathways and decision rules.

**Customers** decide on the basis of perceived value, perceived risk and identity. In business-to-business markets, the "customer" is a buying committee whose members hold different fears: the finance lead fears overspending, the technical lead fears failure, the executive sponsor fears reputational embarrassment. Each fear can be addressed truthfully with its own evidence.

**Employees and candidates** decide where to work, how hard to work and whether to stay, based on beliefs about mission, fairness, stability and prospects. They are also the organization's most credible messengers and its most likely leak path.

**Investors and lenders** price narrative as well as numbers. Their beliefs about strategy, management credibility and risk affect the cost of capital. Public companies must also manage this audience within securities disclosure rules such as Regulation FD (17 CFR Part 243).

**Competitors** are an audience too. Their beliefs about a firm's intentions, capabilities and resolve shape their pricing, product and entry decisions. Clear, public, truthful signals—such as a credible commitment to a market—can deter wasteful competition. This is the lawful commercial counterpart of the military idea of shaping an opponent's decisions, and Section 2 explores it.

**Regulators and policymakers** form views about an industry from evidence, testimony and public sentiment. Engaging them lawfully means transparent advocacy, registered lobbying where required, and honest data.

A sixth group cuts across all five: **intermediaries**—journalists, analysts, creators, review platforms and, increasingly, AI assistants that summarize what the web says about a company. Intermediaries are force multipliers and cannot be controlled. They can only be informed, engaged and, where they err, corrected.

## 1.5 The Doctrinal Lineage in Brief

Military and strategic traditions worldwide have long recognized the decisive role of influencing how opponents perceive situations and make decisions. Russian military thought developed the concept of reflexive control—the process of conveying specially prepared information so that an adversary voluntarily makes decisions favorable to the initiator. Chinese military writings have elaborated cognitive domain operations as a distinct battlespace aimed at shaping cognition, will, and decision-making across peacetime and conflict. These approaches treat human cognition itself as contested terrain and provide doctrinal foundations that can be adapted to corporate environments.
