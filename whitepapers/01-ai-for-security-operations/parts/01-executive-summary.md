# Executive Summary

*By William Aaron Turner, Founder and President, Infinitum Corporation*

Let's start with a number. **31%.**

That is the share of breaches that began with the exploitation of a software vulnerability in Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, up from 20% the year before. It is the first time in the report's 19-year history that exploitation has beaten stolen credentials as the top way in (Verizon DBIR, 2026). The same report found that only 26% of the most critical known-exploited vulnerabilities were fully fixed. The median time to fix them: 43 days.

Now put yourself in the seat of a security leader at a mid-size organization. You have a small team. You have a long list of things to patch. You have alerts stacking up, vendors calling, and a board asking what you are doing about AI. Meanwhile your adversaries have lower costs, faster tools, and no HR department.

This paper is about that situation. It is not a hype piece, and it is not a vendor brochure. It is a practical look at how a mid-size organization can use artificial intelligence to strengthen its security operations, without handing control to a black box and without creating a new attack surface along the way.

I am going to look at the problem the way an irregular warfare analyst looks at it. I want to be careful about what that means, so here it is plainly. I am not talking about weapons, and I am not talking about offense. Irregular warfare, in the public doctrine, is about contests where the adversary avoids a head-on fight, works below the threshold of a decisive response, relies on indirect and asymmetric methods, uses proxies and criminal networks, and competes for influence and legitimacy over time (DoD, 2020). That is a surprisingly good description of what most organizations face every day in cyberspace. The lens does not make the threat sound more dramatic. It makes the defense more disciplined.

## What the evidence says

Four findings carry most of the weight in this paper.

**First, the adversary is persistent and fast, and mostly ordinary.** Vulnerability exploitation and phishing remain the leading entry points. ENISA's 2025 Threat Landscape put phishing at about 60% of observed intrusions and vulnerability exploitation at 21.3% (ENISA, 2025). Verizon's data adds that generative AI is helping threat actors at different stages of an attack. But most AI-assisted malware in its dataset mapped to well-known techniques, with a median of 55 existing malware examples performing the same functions (Verizon DBIR, 2026). The adversary is not doing magic. The adversary is doing the usual things faster and cheaper.

**Second, the criminal ecosystem is the proxy force.** Ransomware was present in 48% of breaches in Verizon's dataset. Third-party involvement also reached 48% of breaches, up 60% from the prior year (Verizon DBIR, 2026). In IW terms, that is an ecosystem of specialists, suppliers and intermediaries that makes an attack cheap to launch and hard to trace to a single actor.

**Third, defenders who use AI and automation extensively report better outcomes, with caveats.** IBM and Ponemon Institute found that breached organizations using security AI and automation extensively averaged USD 4.00 million per breach, against USD 5.93 million for those using none. They also identified and contained breaches 65 days faster (IBM/Ponemon, 2026). That is an association from a benchmark study, not a guarantee. And only 36% of breached organizations used these tools extensively.

**Fourth, AI is also terrain.** IBM reported that 21% of organizations had a security incident involving an AI model or application, and that among those with an AI-related breach, 92% lacked proper AI access controls (IBM/Ponemon, 2026). Shadow AI, meaning employees using unapproved AI tools, is now a data-loss channel in its own right (Verizon DBIR, 2026). The lesson is old and simple. When you adopt a new capability, you create new ground that someone else can contest.

## What I recommend

Here is the approach, in six moves. The paper explains each one.

1. **Govern first.** Name an owner. Write the rules. Decide what data may go where.
2. **Measure a baseline.** If you cannot measure today, you cannot prove tomorrow.
3. **Pilot with analysts in the loop.** AI proposes. A person decides.
4. **Point AI at exposure.** Use it where adversaries are most likely to get in: unfixed vulnerabilities, weak identity, forgotten assets.
5. **Secure the AI itself.** Protect the models, the data, the connections and the identities around them.
6. **Test, then widen.** Use authorized testing to prove the controls, and extend automation only as the evidence earns it.

Three ideas from the irregular warfare lens run through all six: **persistence** (assume the adversary will be back), **resilience** (plan to operate while contested), and **deterrence by denial** (make success so costly and uncertain that most adversaries move on to an easier target).

I should say what this paper does not do. It does not promise results for any particular organization. The statistics belong to the sources cited, and every one carries its source in the text. The analysis and the conclusions are mine.

All data comes from the public record. Don't take my word for it. Read the sources, and then decide.
