The venture fund in Tysons that shapes America's battlefield technology

Most Washingtonians have driven past In-Q-Tel without knowing it. The not-for-profit venture capital firm, set up by the CIA in 1999 and based in the Tysons area of Fairfax County, has made more than 800 investments in its first quarter-century. Its public filings show fiscal 2025 revenue of about $132 million, most of it government grants and contributions. Few institutions in the region have done more, more quietly, to decide which commercial technologies reach intelligence analysts and, increasingly, the battlefield.

I have read through In-Q-Tel's filings, portfolio announcements and the public reporting on its companies. The picture that emerges is not a shadowy conspiracy. It is something more ordinary and more consequential: a small public-private pipeline that turns government requirements into startup business plans, with very little public accounting of results.

The mechanics are straightforward. In-Q-Tel's Interface Center translates the needs of intelligence agencies into commercial problem sets. Startups that can address them receive technology-development agreements, typically in the range of $500,000 to $3 million, alongside equity investment. The model works because it lets agencies buy into innovation at the speed of venture capital rather than the speed of procurement. The firm's early bet on Palantir, made in the mid-2000s, is the best-known example of how a modest check can help shape an entire industry.

The portfolio leans heavily toward enterprise software, artificial intelligence and data analytics, followed by cybersecurity, semiconductors, space, energy, biotechnology and robotics. Several of those bets are now visible in the war in Ukraine through public reporting: satellite-radar companies such as Capella Space and ICEYE, Palantir's software work, and the defense startup Anduril, in which In-Q-Tel's investment is documented. Let me be precise about what the record shows. There is no open-source evidence of In-Q-Tel subsidiaries operating inside Ukraine. What the record shows is funding and technology, not people on the ground.

That distinction is exactly why the model deserves more public scrutiny, not less. When taxpayer money seeds companies whose products later shape a war, Americans should be able to see, at least in aggregate, how that money performs. Which investments delivered capabilities the government actually adopted? Which were written off? How are conflicts handled when In-Q-Tel's trustees, advisers or alumni sit on the boards of portfolio companies that later win federal contracts?

None of this requires exposing classified programs. Other public investors already publish more. University endowments disclose asset-class returns. State pension funds publish holdings. In-Q-Tel could release an annual, unclassified report covering the share of investments that led to government adoption, the share that failed, the follow-on private capital its bets attracted and its conflict-of-interest procedures. Congress, which funds the arrangement, could ask for exactly that.

There is a regional interest too. Northern Virginia's economy runs on the intersection of government demand and private technology. In-Q-Tel is one of the clearest expressions of that model, and its success has been copied by the Pentagon's own innovation offices and by allied governments. If the region wants that model to keep its public legitimacy, it should welcome transparency about how it works.

The broader lesson is about how wars now get equipped. The decisive tools in Ukraine, from commercial satellite imagery to data platforms, often began as venture bets made in office parks around the Beltway. That is not inherently sinister. It is how modern defense technology is built. But a democracy should understand the pipeline that builds it, and right now most residents of the region that hosts it do not.
