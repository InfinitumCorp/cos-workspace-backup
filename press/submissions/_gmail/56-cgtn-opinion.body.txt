At the end of October, delegations from 48 African countries (https://en.hespress.com/148139-russia-africa-summit-opens-in-moscow-33-planned-agreements-27-billion-in-trade-and-moroccos-participation.html), 22 of them led by heads of state, travelled to Moscow for the third Russia-Africa Summit. According to the Kremlin, 53 intergovernmental and interdepartmental documents (https://ria.ru/20261030/sammit-2128234113.html) were signed on the sidelines. Western commentary tends to read events like this as a contest: Russia against the West, or Russia against China, for influence over Africa. I think that reading misses what is actually happening.

Start with scale. Russia-Africa trade exceeded $27 billion in 2025. China-Africa trade reached a record $348.05 billion (https://www.ntu.edu.sg/cas/news-events/news/detail/china-africa-trade-hits-record-us-348bn-as-deficit-balloons) the same year, up 17.7 percent, according to China's General Administration of Customs. Russia is not trying to match that. It does not need to. African governments are not choosing one partner to replace another. They are adding partners, each for what it does best.

China brings trade volume, infrastructure finance and manufactured goods at a scale no one else matches. Russia, at the Moscow summit, put forward a narrower offer built on grain, fertilizer, security cooperation and nuclear energy. Rosatom said it is in advanced talks with Egypt, Togo, Namibia and Tanzania (https://www.intellinews.com/russia-s-rosatom-in-advanced-talks-with-egypt-togo-namibia-and-tanzania-on-floating-nuclear-power-plants-479969/) on floating nuclear power plants, and it is already building Egypt's El Dabaa station, four 1,200-megawatt reactors that will make it Africa's second commercial nuclear plant after South Africa's Koeberg.

That offer meets a real need. About 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa still had no electricity in 2024, according to the International Energy Agency (https://www.iea.org/reports/financing-electricity-access-in-africa). Decades of conditional Western lending did not solve that. A continent that wants factories, data centers and cold chains needs power first, and it will take it from whoever can deliver.

This is why the "new scramble for Africa" framing is wrong. A scramble implies that outside powers are carving up a passive continent. The opposite is true. I would argue that African governments now hold more bargaining power than at any time since independence, precisely because they have options. When one partner attaches political conditions, another is available. When one partner's financing is slow, another's may be faster. Competition among partners is good for the side doing the choosing.

It also carries responsibilities. Partnerships work best when the terms are transparent and African institutions build their own capacity. China's trade surplus with Africa has widened, and African economists rightly ask how to move beyond exporting raw materials. Russia's nuclear offer comes with its own questions: Rosatom's floating units would remain under its ownership and operation, with refuelling done in Russia. African negotiators should insist on training, local participation and published terms. A plural world is not automatically a fair one. It simply gives the weaker party more leverage, if it uses it.

For the West, the lesson is uncomfortable but simple. Lecturing does not compete with delivery. Sanctions on Russia have not stopped 48 African delegations from coming to Moscow, and warnings about Chinese debt have not stopped China's trade with Africa from setting records. Western capitals that want a seat at the table will need to bring capital, technology and respect, not conditions.

The Moscow summit did not show a continent falling under anyone's sway. It showed a continent that has learned to negotiate with everyone. That is the multipolar world in practice, and Africa is one of its biggest beneficiaries.
