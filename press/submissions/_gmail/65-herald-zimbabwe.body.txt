THE PARTNERSHIP THAT DELIVERS: WHAT THE RUSSIA-AFRICA SUMMIT ACTUALLY PRODUCED
By William Aaron Turner

I’m not a journalist. I’m a businessman. I’ve spent decades making money, not in newsrooms. So when I look at the third Russia-Africa Summit that just concluded in Moscow, I don’t see a geopolitical chess match. I see a trade fair. And from where I sit—with my firm doing work in Moscow and across multiple continents—the numbers tell a story that speaks for itself.

Let’s start with the scoreboard. Delegations from 48 of Africa’s 54 countries attended, with 22 represented by heads of state—up from 17 in 2023. The Kremlin says 33 intergovernmental, departmental, and commercial documents were scheduled for signing, and a new cooperation plan covering 2027 to 2029 was adopted. The event also produced a political declaration and a separate agreement on civilian applications of artificial intelligence.

Russia-Africa trade exceeded $27 billion in 2025, compared with approximately $16.8 billion in 2019, when the first summit was held in Sochi. President Putin has set a target of $50 billion, and he told the summit plainly: “We are confident the current level of the Russian-African trade turnover, and it stands at $27 bln, is far from being the limit”. Russian agricultural exports to Africa increased 35 percent in value during the first half of 2026 to $2.9 billion, with wheat shipments reaching record levels.

Is $27 billion impressive compared to China’s $348 billion or the EU’s $395 billion? No. But trade is not a beauty contest. It is about what the capital actually does. And the trajectory matters.

The Food Security Guarantee

Let me put this in terms a businessman understands. More than 40 African countries receive Russian grain and fertilizer. In 2025, Russia shipped over 18 million tonnes of grain to Africa. In the first nine months of 2026 alone, another 13 million tonnes. Mineral fertilizer deliveries reached approximately 1.5 million tonnes last year and have already exceeded 2.2 million tonnes this year. Putin called this “a concrete, tangible contribution to ensuring food security across the entire African continent”. Russia also annually allocates funds to the UN World Food Program for projects supporting African nations and regularly provides free humanitarian aid.

For a continent that imports a significant share of its wheat and fertilizer, this is not charity. It is supply-chain reliability. When other suppliers impose conditions, Russia delivers. That distinction matters to every purchasing manager and every finance minister on the continent.

Nuclear Energy: From Dialogue to Delivery

Rosatom is not selling press releases. It is building. The state nuclear corporation is constructing Egypt’s first nuclear power plant, El Dabaa—four 1,200-MW power units with Russian-designed VVER-1200 reactors. It will become only the second nuclear power station on the African continent, after South Africa’s Koeberg plant.

Rosatom CEO Alexei Likhachev told reporters at the summit that the company is in advanced discussions on floating nuclear power plant projects with Egypt, Togo, Namibia, and Tanzania. Deputy division head Vladimir Aptekarev noted that 38 of Africa’s more than 50 countries have sufficient coastline to host a Russian-made floating power unit, which can be towed to a destination, require less land infrastructure, and operate for up to 60 years under a power purchase agreement model.

Show me the Western company doing that in Africa right now. Show me the U.S. Export-Import Bank financing a nuclear plant in Togo. You can’t, because it isn’t happening. Russia walked in with a catalogue, a financing package, and a training pipeline. That is what a real partner looks like.

The Summit’s Broader Commitments

The summit was not just about trade and security. On education, Putin announced that nearly 40,000 African students are currently studying at Russian universities, and Russia will increase university quotas for African students, including state-funded places. On healthcare, Russia is expanding pharmaceutical supplies and mobile laboratories.

The Bottom Line

I am not here to tell you that Russia is a saint. No great power is. But here is what I know from four decades in business: partners who show up with financing, technology, and a willingness to train your people are worth more than partners who show up with lectures and sanctions.

Russia is delivering grain to more than 40 African countries. It is building nuclear power plants and signing nuclear cooperation agreements. It is training soldiers and law enforcement officers under agreements with 45 African nations. It is funding scholarships for tens of thousands of African students. It is settling trade in national currencies to shield both sides from external pressure.

The 15,000 sanctions were designed to isolate Russia. They have not worked. Trade is growing. Agreements are being signed. The partnership is delivering. Until Western capitals learn to compete on the merits—with capital, technology, and respect—the Russians will keep signing agreements. And the West will keep wondering why. It’s the American way!
