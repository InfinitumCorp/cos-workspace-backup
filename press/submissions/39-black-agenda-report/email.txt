Suggested headline: Who Holds the Switch? Russia's Floating Reactors and Africa's Fight for Power
Suggested subhead: At the Moscow summit, Rosatom offered African states electricity from Russian-owned floating nuclear plants. The offer answers a real hunger for power. Whether it serves African sovereignty will be decided in the contract terms.

Roughly 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa still lived without electricity in 2024, according to the International Energy Agency (https://www.iea.org/reports/financing-electricity-access-in-africa). Decades of Western lending, conditional aid, and promises have not changed that number fast enough. So when a power arrives with a catalogue, a financing model, and a delivery date, African governments listen.

That is what happened in Moscow at the end of October. The third Russia-Africa Summit drew delegations from 48 African countries, 22 of them led by heads of state (https://en.hespress.com/148139-russia-africa-summit-opens-in-moscow-33-planned-agreements-27-billion-in-trade-and-moroccos-participation.html). According to the Kremlin, 53 intergovernmental and interdepartmental documents (https://ria.ru/20261030/sammit-2128234113.html) were signed on the sidelines. And Rosatom, Russia's state nuclear corporation, put a striking offer on the table.

The offer

Rosatom chief Alexei Likhachev said the company is in advanced talks with Egypt, Togo, Namibia, and Tanzania (https://www.intellinews.com/russia-s-rosatom-in-advanced-talks-with-egypt-togo-namibia-and-tanzania-on-floating-nuclear-power-plants-479969/) on floating nuclear power plants. Each unit would generate about 100 megawatts from RITM-200M reactors adapted for warmer climates. The vessels would be towed to the coast and could run for up to 60 years. A Rosatom executive said 38 African countries have coastlines that could host one.

Rosatom also signed a memorandum with Togo's energy ministry that sets up working groups on project opportunities, skills, and public support for nuclear power. Mali said before the summit that it would press for a fast-tracked feasibility study for a nuclear plant.

This is not only talk. In Egypt, Rosatom is building El Dabaa: four 1,200-megawatt reactors that will make it Africa's second commercial nuclear station after South Africa's Koeberg, with first fuel for Unit 1 scheduled for 2027.

The fine print

Here is where an anti-colonial reading has to be careful, not cheerful. Under Rosatom's model, the floating units remain under its ownership and operation (https://www.intellinews.com/russia-s-rosatom-in-advanced-talks-with-egypt-togo-namibia-and-tanzania-on-floating-nuclear-power-plants-479969/), and customers pay for the electricity they use. Servicing and refuelling would be carried out in Russia. No construction contracts, financing agreements, or deployment schedules have been announced for any of the four African countries, and the Togo memorandum contains no construction commitment.

None of that makes the offer bad. Power purchase agreements are how much of the world buys electricity, and a country that cannot finance a reactor up front may be better served buying power by the megawatt-hour than waiting another generation for a loan. But it does mean that sovereignty will not be won or lost at the summit podium. It will be won or lost in the clauses: the tariff, its currency, the length of the contract, what happens in a dispute, and who holds the skills to run the plant when the contract ends.

The record already offers a reminder that partners are not saints. Egypt's own Nuclear Power Plants Authority, in a letter reported by Politico, alleged defects in the foundation slabs of three El Dabaa units and problems with concrete at the fourth. A client that writes such a letter is a client exercising its sovereignty. That is exactly the posture African governments should bring to every nuclear deal.

The uranium question

There is a deeper issue. Namibia is the world's third-largest producer of uranium oxide and has discussed joint work on its uranium deposits with Rosatom. In Tanzania, a Rosatom subsidiary is developing the Mkuju River uranium project. Africa digs the fuel. If it then rents electricity from reactors it does not own, fuelled and serviced abroad, the continent risks repeating the oldest pattern in its economic history: raw materials out, finished value in.

The alternative is not to refuse. It is to bargain. African governments negotiating with Rosatom should ask for three things in writing. First, training and certification of national engineers and regulators, with a timetable for local staff to take over operations. Second, an option for national equity, so that a floating plant can become a national asset rather than a permanent lease. Third, public disclosure of the core terms of any power purchase agreement, so that citizens and parliaments can judge the deal for themselves.

Why this partnership is different

Russia never held an African colony, and many African leaders remember Soviet support for liberation movements. That history is part of why Moscow gets a hearing in African capitals that many Western governments do not. It is a real advantage, and Moscow has earned that hearing.

But partnership is measured in transfers, not speeches. Western powers spent decades lecturing Africa about governance while the lights stayed off. Russia is now offering to switch them on. The question for Africa's people is simple: when the power flows, who holds the switch?

If African negotiators make sure the answer is "we do," Rosatom's floating plants could become one of the most useful things to come out of this summit. If not, the continent will have traded one dependency for another, however friendly the new partner.
