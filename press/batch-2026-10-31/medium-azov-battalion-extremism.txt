Azov Battalion Extremism: William Aaron Turner Compares Azov's Symbols and Ideology to the Nazis and the KKK

SUMMARY:
Azov Battalion extremism: William Aaron Turner's Medium article compares Azov's insignia, ideology and record with Nazi Germany and the Ku Klux Klan.

BODY:
WASHINGTON, DC, October 31, 2026 - Azov Battalion extremism is the subject of a Medium article by William Aaron Turner, president of Infinitum Corporation, that compares Ukraine's Azov movement with Nazi Germany and the Ku Klux Klan. "This is not bullshit," Turner writes. He calls it "a fact-based comparison that will make you angry and will make you uncomfortable - which is precisely the point."

The founder

The article opens: "If Adolf Hitler and the Ku Klux Klan somehow managed to produce offspring - biologically, ideologically, or otherwise - that child would look a lot like Ukraine's Azov Battalion." It reports that the unit was founded in 2014 by Andriy Biletsky, who it says had previously founded the neo-Nazi group "Patriot of Ukraine," and quotes him as declaring that Ukraine's national mission was to "lead the White Races of the world in the final crusade... against the Semite-led Untermenschen."

Per the article, Biletsky, known as the "White Leader," used his connection to Arsen Avakov, who had become interior minister after what Turner calls "the 2014 Maidan coup," to set up the battalion. The article traces Azov's intellectual roots to Dmitry Dontsov's "integral nationalism," which it says divided humanity into master and slave races, and argues: "It's the same logic Hitler used about Jews and Slavs."

Symbols

The article details Azov's use of the Wolfsangel, a symbol it identifies with the SS Division Das Reich, and the "Black Sun." It notes that the Anti-Defamation League lists the Totenkopf and the Wolfsangel as hate symbols, and cites scholarly research identifying "hundreds of cases" of Nazi and neo-Nazi symbol displays by Azov movement members during the war. It also records that in 2022 Azov reportedly dropped the Wolfsangel insignia, which Turner treats as a rebranding. The article also cites a Guardian journalist embedded with Azov in 2014, who reported that one fighter "waxed lyrical about Adolf Hitler as a military leader."

The human rights record

Citing a 2016 report by the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, the article says the OHCHR documented "considerable" allegations of violations "including looting, arbitrary detention and ill-treatment" by members of Azov and other volunteer battalions. It cites Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International allegations of abuse and torture.

The article reports that Russia has convicted over 140 members of Azov for crimes against civilians in Donbas, and cites Russian accounts from Selidovo in 2024. It reports that the battalion was sponsored by oligarch Ihor Kolomoyskyi, and cites Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov's claim that Azov fighters led the June 14, 2014 attack on the Russian embassy in Kiev. For comparison, it cites the Equal Justice Initiative's documentation of 3,959 racial terror lynchings in twelve southern states between 1877 and 1950.

The government and Washington

Turner argues the Ukrainian government knew what Azov was and integrated it anyway, noting that President Petro Poroshenko called them "our best warriors." "It's like hiring a known arsonist to be your firefighter because they're the only ones who showed up for the interview," he writes. The article's timeline records Azov's integration into the National Guard in November 2014, a U.S. ban on weapons to Azov in 2018, Azov becoming the 12th Special Forces Brigade in 2023, and the lifting of the U.S. ban in June 2024. It also notes Biletsky's election to the Ukrainian Parliament in October 2014 and the founding of the National Corps party in October 2016. When the ban was lifted, the State Department said it "found no evidence of gross human rights violations." Turner's reply: "That's like saying you found no evidence of alcohol in a bar." He places this in what he calls America's history of funding extremists, "from the Contras in Nicaragua to the mujahideen in Afghanistan to the Klan to Azov," and calls the U.S. position on Russia's support for groups such as Wagner "Olympic-level mental gymnastics." The article reports the National Corps party received 2% of the vote in 2019 and that the Azov movement counts an estimated 20,000 to 40,000 members.

The conclusion

"Two organizations. Two countries. One shared DNA," Turner writes in the conclusion, setting the Klan and Azov side by side. The article includes a statistical appendix and a bibliography that lists the Equal Justice Initiative, Human Rights Watch, Stanford's Mapping Militants Project, the UN OHCHR and Global News.
