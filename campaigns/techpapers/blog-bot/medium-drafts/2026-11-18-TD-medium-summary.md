Nobody wins this fight in a day.

Defending a network is not one dramatic battle. It is a long grind of small, cheap, constant pressure: scans that never stop, phishing in bursts, flood traffic that lasts minutes and returns tomorrow, an edge device waiting weeks for a patch. Attrition—cost, endurance, replacement—is the right word. The side that absorbs, repairs, adapts and keeps operating outlasts the side that cannot.

*Trenches and Drones: Network Defense for a War of Attrition*, Whitepaper No. 2 from Infinitum Corporation®, is William Aaron Turner’s practical guide for mid-size organizations living in that contest. Irregular warfare is the analytic lens: indirect, hard-to-attribute, asymmetric pressure that raises an adversary’s costs. The modern battlefield, as public analysts describe it, supplies the analogy—depth, cheap mass, jammed and spoofed signals, sensor transparency, fragile logistics—not a claim that your network is a front line. The paper takes no side, makes no claim about any party, and leaves out casualty, loss and performance figures because a cyber defender does not need them.

Five fronts structure the argument. **Trenches** map to depth: segmentation, identity checks at every line, and an implicit trust zone kept as small as possible. **Drones** map to cheap mass: automated scanning, credential stuffing, denial-of-service bursts—answered with cheap, repeatable controls, not one exquisite box. **Electronic warfare** maps to trust under attack: time sources, name resolution, identities, voices. Keep a second source; verify out of band. **Sensors** maps to exposure: assume the outside can see; shrink what need not be visible; build your own telemetry. **Supply lines** maps to third parties and platforms: the rear is not safe. Command and people cut across all five—decision rights, out-of-band communications, staffing for year two.

Public incident and breach research underlines why endurance matters: volume is mostly cheap; the real damage often sits in gaps that stay open; breach lifecycles measured in many months are expensive. The recommendations are seven sequenced moves a small team can actually run: map the ground; dig in; close the cheap doors; keep a second source; reduce your signature; plan for the rear to burn; decentralize the decisions. None of them needs a miracle product. All of them need a campaign mindset—measure against your own baseline, and break the plan in rehearsal before a real incident does.


The paper is also clear about where the analogy breaks. A network is not terrain; you can restore a server from backup in ways a lost position does not allow. Most pressure on a mid-size organization comes from criminals, opportunists and automated tools, not armies. Battlefield statistics do not transfer to your firewall, which is why the paper’s numbers come from breach and incident research instead. Do not mistake analogy for instruction: this is not a call for private offensive action.

Inside the paper: a 30/60/90-day plan, a 12-month roadmap, a 30-item self-assessment, and three illustrative scenarios marked hypothetical. Every figure belongs to a cited source. The work stays defensive and strategic. It is not a weapons paper, not targeting guidance, and not a vendor list.
