The signal is gone.

Navigation says one thing. The clock says another. The voice on the phone sounds exactly like the chief executive. That is not a war-movie beat. It is a fair description of the contested information environment mid-size security teams already work in—spoofed identities, jammed telemetry, and adversaries who hide inside normal administrative noise.

*Dead Reckoning: AI for Security Operations*, Whitepaper No. 1 from Infinitum Corporation®, is a practical look at where AI helps a cyber security program, where it hurts, and how a mid-size organization can start without giving up control. The author, William Aaron Turner, Founder and President, reads the problem through irregular warfare: competition below the threshold of open conflict, using indirect, deniable and asymmetric means. You do not need to care about doctrine to use the lens. You only need to notice that the people probing your network behave the way that literature describes—probe, blend in, pick the cheapest door, wait.

Public analysis of the modern battlefield supplies the comparison, not a claim that your servers are a front line. The paper takes no side and makes no claim about any party. Persistence beats one clever move. Cheap mass pressures expensive systems. Sensors watch constantly. Signals can be jammed and spoofed. Supply lines and suppliers are terrain. The title is a navigation term: when the feed fails, you work from last known position, heading, speed, time—and a second source you still trust. That is the posture the paper wants for security operations.

Three findings from the public record frame the capacity problem. Attackers are getting faster and cheaper; vulnerability exploitation has become a leading path into breaches, while remediation of known-exploited flaws often lags. AI and automation are associated, on average, with better breach outcomes in industry studies—averages, not guarantees. And AI itself is a new attack surface when access control and governance are weak. Mid-size organizations sit in the awkward middle: interesting enough to hit, rarely staffed for a full around-the-clock security operations center plus separate intelligence and vulnerability teams.

The paper’s answer is deliberately unglamorous. Govern first. Measure a baseline. Pilot with analysts in the loop. Point AI at exposure. Secure the AI itself. Widen automation only as evidence earns it. Six use cases get sustained treatment: alert triage, threat-intelligence enrichment, vulnerability prioritization, log analysis, reporting, and deception and early warning. Governance is structured under the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. People and metrics get their own sections, because a tool that produces confident wrong answers at machine speed does not reduce workload—it relocates it to cleanup.


Seven lessons from the battlefield chapter turn into program checks: plan for a campaign rather than a single incident; answer volume with layers; know what is visible about you; verify critical signals out of band; expect controls to decay and re-test them; treat suppliers as rear area; pre-authorize decisions and keep a communications path that does not depend on the network you are defending. AI belongs in that picture as cheap mass on the defender’s side—triage and summarization at volume—with a person on the decision.

A 90-day plan and a 12-month roadmap walk through that sequence, with a maturity self-assessment teams can run themselves. Illustrative scenarios are labeled hypothetical. Every statistic is tied to a source the author opened. The standard the paper holds an AI program to is resilience: absorb a blow, keep working, recover—not merely get busier.
