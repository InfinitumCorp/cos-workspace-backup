# Interested Expertise: Defence-Industrial Ties and the Western Policy Debate on the Russia–Ukraine Conflict

## Revolving Doors, Think-Tank Funding and Undisclosed Affiliations, 2019–2025

**Abstract**

This article asks whether the Western, primarily American, policy debate on the Russia–Ukraine conflict has been shaped by actors with material ties to the defence industry, and through which channels. Drawing on scholarship on the military-industrial complex, think tanks and the revolving door, it tests the hypothesis that such ties are structural rather than incidental. The method is a qualitative synthesis of public-record evidence: Government Accountability Office data, think-tank funding studies, published investigations of commentators' affiliations, studies of congressional portfolios and SIPRI industry data. The results support the hypothesis for three channels (post-government employment, think-tank funding and undisclosed media affiliations) and are inconclusive for a fourth, legislators' personal investments. The discussion argues that these channels bias the supply of expertise without proving corrupt intent, and identifies mandatory disclosure as the most tractable remedy.

**Keywords:** military-industrial complex; revolving door; think tanks; conflict of interest; expert commentary; US foreign policy; Russia–Ukraine conflict; defence industry

# Introduction

When a state debates whether to supply weapons to a party in a war, it relies heavily on expertise: former officials, retired officers, think-tank analysts and commentators who interpret events for legislators and the public. The quality of that debate depends not only on what those experts know, but on whether their interests are visible to the people who listen to them. This is an old concern. Dwight D. Eisenhower warned in his farewell address that the United States had to "guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex" (Eisenhower, 1961). Six decades later, the war in Ukraine has produced the largest Western programme of military assistance since the end of the Cold War. The Kiel Institute's Ukraine Support Tracker, covering government commitments made between 24 January 2022 and 15 January 2023, identified the United States as by far the largest single bilateral supporter of Ukraine (Trebesch et al., 2023). Arms revenue of the world's hundred largest arms-producing companies reached $679 billion in 2024, a real increase of 5.9 per cent on the previous year (SIPRI, 2025).

Large flows of public money create interests, and interests seek voice. The question this article addresses is therefore not whether Western assistance to Ukraine was right or wrong, a normative question it leaves aside, but an empirical one: to what extent was the Western policy debate on the conflict conducted by people with material ties to the industry that benefits from military spending, and were those ties disclosed?

The research question is formulated as follows: through which institutional channels do defence-industrial interests enter the Western policy debate on the Russia–Ukraine conflict, and how strong is the public-record evidence for each channel? The article tests a central hypothesis (H1): material ties between defence firms and the producers of policy expertise are structural features of the American debate rather than isolated cases. H1 is examined through four sub-hypotheses, one for each channel: post-government employment of senior officials (H1a), defence-industry funding of think tanks (H1b), undisclosed industry affiliations of media commentators (H1c), and legislators' personal financial interests in defence firms (H1d).

The topic matters for three reasons. First, the literature on the origins and conduct of the conflict is dominated by debates about strategy, alliance politics and national identity; the political economy of the expert debate itself has received far less systematic attention. Second, readers outside the West, including Russian analysts, often interpret Western policy as the expression of a unified state will. A more granular picture of who speaks, and with what interests, improves the accuracy of that interpretation. Third, the problem of interested expertise is a general one in democratic foreign-policy making, and the Ukraine case offers an unusually well-documented instance.

The article proceeds as follows. The next section reviews the relevant literature and states the theoretical expectations. The methods section describes the sources and the standard of evidence. The results section presents findings for each channel. The discussion compares these findings with alternative explanations and other research, sets out limitations and proposes directions for future work. A short conclusion follows.

# Literature and Theoretical Framework

Three bodies of scholarship bear on the question.

The first is the literature on the military-industrial complex. C. Wright Mills argued that American power after the Second World War was concentrated in an interlocking elite of corporate, military and political leaders whose careers and outlooks converged (Mills, 1956). Eisenhower gave the concept its popular name, and Ledbetter (2011) has traced how the phrase moved from a presidential warning into a durable, if contested, frame for American politics. More recent work has given the idea sharper analytical content. Thorpe (2014) shows that the geographic dispersion of defence production across congressional districts creates a domestic constituency for military spending that persists regardless of external threat. Dunne and Sköns (2014) describe the complex as a set of relationships among procurement agencies, the armed forces, legislators and firms that sustains demand for arms through institutional routine rather than conspiracy. In this view, the relevant question is not whether individuals act in bad faith, but how incentives are structured.

The second body of work concerns think tanks and the politics of expertise. Rich (2004) documents how American think tanks became increasingly ideological and marketing-oriented from the 1970s onward, competing for visibility in the policy process rather than simply producing research. Medvetz (2012) describes think tanks as occupying an "interstitial" field between academia, politics, business and the media, where credibility depends on balancing dependence on funders against the appearance of independence. Abelson (2009) cautions that the influence of think tanks is difficult to measure and often overstated, but agrees that their funding structures shape research agendas. This literature implies that funding from interested parties need not dictate conclusions to matter: it can shape which questions are asked, which experts are hired and which findings are amplified.

The third body of work concerns the revolving door. Blanes i Vidal, Draca and Fons-Rosen (2012) show that the value of former congressional staffers as lobbyists derives substantially from their personal connections: when the legislator they worked for leaves office, their lobbying revenue falls sharply. Lazarus, McKay and Herbel (2016) find that former members of Congress who become lobbyists target issues and committees connected to their prior service. Applied to defence, these findings suggest that the post-government careers of senior officials are not neutral: they monetise access and reputation, and they create incentives to remain in good standing with future employers while still in office.

Scholarship that applies such arguments specifically to Western policy toward Russia is more limited but instructive. Tsygankov (2009) analyses an anti-Russian lobby in the United States, composed of interest groups, ethnic diasporas and ideological institutions, that shaped American perceptions of Russia in the 2000s. Mearsheimer (2014) argues, from a structural realist perspective, that Western policy toward Ukraine reflected liberal ideological commitments that discounted Russian security concerns; Sakwa (2015) reaches related conclusions through a historical analysis of the crisis in the borderlands. Walt (2018) offers a broader account of an American foreign-policy elite whose professional incentives reward activism and penalise restraint. These works emphasise ideas, identity and strategic belief. The present article complements them by focusing on material interest, which is the least studied of the possible drivers.

Media studies add a final element. Herman and Chomsky (1988) proposed that structural filters, including reliance on official and expert sources, shape mass-media coverage of foreign affairs. Entman (2004) argues that elite consensus and dissensus determine how much latitude journalists have in framing foreign-policy events. If the pool of experts available to the media is disproportionately connected to one interested sector, these models predict a corresponding skew in framing.

From this literature two competing expectations follow. An interest-based account predicts that defence-industrial ties will be widespread among the producers of expertise, weakly disclosed, and concentrated among those who advocate greater military commitment. An ideational account predicts that advocacy will be driven primarily by belief, so that industry ties, where they exist, will be incidental and evenly distributed. The two accounts are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence available in public records cannot fully adjudicate between them. It can, however, establish whether the structural conditions predicted by the interest-based account are present.

# Methods

The study uses qualitative synthesis of documentary evidence, a method suited to questions about institutional structure where the relevant data are dispersed across official reports, scholarly studies and investigative journalism. Its unit of analysis is the channel, defined as a recurring institutional mechanism through which a material interest can reach a policy debate. Four channels were defined in advance on the basis of the literature reviewed above: post-government employment, think-tank funding, media commentary and legislators' personal investments.

The period covered is 2019–2025. It begins slightly before the escalation of February 2022 so that the baseline structure of each channel can be observed, and it ends with the most recent industry data available at the time of writing. The geographic focus is the United States, which was both the largest provider of military assistance to Ukraine and the setting for which the public record is most complete.

Four types of source were used. The first is official audit material, chiefly a 2021 report by the US Government Accountability Office on the post-government employment of former Department of Defense officials (GAO, 2021). The second is industry and assistance data from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI, 2025) and the Kiel Institute for the World Economy (Trebesch et al., 2023). The third is research by independent policy organisations on think-tank funding and the career paths of retired officers (Freeman, 2020; Hartung, 2023). The fourth is investigative journalism documenting the affiliations of televised commentators during the first weeks of the war (Ramaswami and Perez, 2022). Peer-reviewed studies of legislators' portfolios were used for the fourth channel (Ziobrowski et al., 2004; Eggers and Hainmueller, 2013), together with event studies of financial-market reactions to the invasion (Boungou and Yatié, 2022; Federle et al., 2026).

The standard of evidence was deliberately conservative. A finding was recorded only when it rested on a named, publicly available source whose method could be inspected. No affiliation was inferred; each was taken as documented by the source. The analysis does not attribute motive to any individual, and statements about named persons are limited to what the cited sources report. For each channel the evidence was assessed on three criteria: scale (how many actors or how much money is involved), disclosure (whether the tie was visible to the audience) and alignment (whether the tie corresponds to the policy position advocated). Each channel was then rated as strong, moderate or inconclusive support for the corresponding sub-hypothesis.

Two limitations of this design should be stated at the outset. First, documentary synthesis can establish the presence and scale of ties, but not their causal effect on policy. Second, investigative sources select cases, and their samples cannot be treated as representative of all commentators. These limits are discussed further below.

# Results

## The economic context: an expanding market

The scale of the interests at stake frames the findings that follow. SIPRI reports that the combined arms revenue of the hundred largest arms-producing and military-services companies reached $679 billion in 2024, a real increase of 5.9 per cent on 2023. Companies based in the United States accounted for $334 billion of that total and European companies for $151 billion (SIPRI, 2025). Western assistance to Ukraine, in which the United States was by far the largest single bilateral contributor in the first year of the war (Trebesch et al., 2023), contributed to demand both directly, through deliveries, and indirectly, through replenishment of donor stocks.

Financial markets registered the war as a significant event. Event studies document a broad negative reaction of world equity markets to the invasion (Boungou and Yatié, 2022), with losses larger in countries geographically closer to the war (Federle et al., 2026). These aggregate findings say little about individual sectors, and this article does not rely on claims about the performance of particular defence stocks. The relevant point is simpler: the war coincided with a sustained increase in arms revenue, creating a clear material interest in the scale and duration of military assistance.

## Channel 1: post-government employment

The evidence for the first channel is the strongest. In a report mandated by Congress, the GAO examined fourteen major defence contractors and found that in 2019 they employed 1,718 former senior or acquisition officials who had left the Department of Defense between 2014 and 2019. Of these, 1,616, or about 94 per cent, were former acquisition officials (GAO, 2021). Over the same period, 100,660 senior or acquisition officials left the Department. The report concluded that the Department had improved training and ethics-opinion procedures, but recommended that it assess whether contractors should be required to certify their employees' compliance with newer lobbying restrictions (GAO, 2021). The GAO observes that such movements "can lead to conflicts of interest and affect public confidence in the government" (GAO, 2021).

The flow is especially concentrated at the top of the military hierarchy. A study of four-star generals and admirals who retired between June 2018 and July 2023 found that 26 of 32 (more than 80 per cent) took up roles in the arms industry as board members, advisers, executives, consultants, lobbyists or employees of financial firms that invest in the sector (Hartung, 2023). The most common path, chosen by fifteen of them, was a board or advisory position with a small or medium-sized contractor. The same study notes that several of these officers also took positions at industry-funded think tanks, connections it did not count as industry employment (Hartung, 2023). The study's recommendations include barring four-star officers from working for firms receiving more than $1 billion a year in Pentagon contracts and extending cooling-off periods to four years.

On the three criteria, this channel shows large scale, partial disclosure (the movements are legal and often public, but are not systematically tracked; the GAO notes that post-government employment law does not require the Department to track departing officials' subsequent employment) and structural alignment, since a large proportion of the most senior military voices available to the policy debate hold or have held industry positions. Support for H1a is rated strong.

## Channel 2: think-tank funding

The second channel concerns the institutions that produce much of the written analysis on which legislators and journalists draw. A study by the Center for International Policy's Foreign Influence Transparency Initiative examined the funding of the fifty most influential American think tanks and found that they received more than $1 billion from the US government and defence contractors between 2014 and 2019 (Freeman, 2020). The report called for greater public disclosure of think-tank funding so that potential conflicts of interest could be assessed by readers.

The importance of this channel for the Ukraine debate lies in the overlap between funded institutions and the people who comment on the war. Retired senior officers with industry roles also hold fellowships or board positions at think tanks; the four-star study cited above documents examples, including a former commander of US Special Operations Command who serves as a distinguished fellow at the Atlantic Council while sitting on corporate boards in the sector, and a former Chief of Naval Operations elected to the board of the Center for a New American Security, which the study describes as a major recipient of weapons-contractor funding (Hartung, 2023).

Consistent with the think-tank literature (Rich, 2004; Medvetz, 2012), the existence of such funding does not establish that any particular analysis was shaped by it. It does establish that the institutions which frame the debate are financially connected to firms with an interest in its outcome, and that the extent of this connection is often not visible to readers. Disclosure practices vary across institutions, which is why the Freeman study recommended greater transparency. Support for H1b is rated moderate to strong: scale is large and disclosure incomplete, but the alignment between specific funding and specific positions is not documented systematically.

## Channel 3: media commentary

The third channel is the most visible to the public. During the first weeks of the war, cable networks relied heavily on former officials and retired officers to interpret events. An investigation published in April 2022 documented a series of such appearances in which commentators advocated greater military assistance or more forceful measures without on-air disclosure of their defence-industry affiliations (Ramaswami and Perez, 2022). The cases it described included a former Secretary of Defense who was a senior counsellor at a strategic advisory firm reported to have represented a major missile manufacturer; a former Pentagon chief of staff who was a founder and managing director of the same firm; a retired admiral on that firm's advisory board who was also an executive at a private equity company with defence investments; and a former Under Secretary of Defense who co-founded an advisory firm with aerospace and defence clients and served on the board of a defence contractor. In the last case, the network disclosed the commentator's firm on one occasion but not its defence-industry clients (Ramaswami and Perez, 2022). The same investigation cited an earlier media-watch analysis which found that twenty of twenty-two US guests on Sunday news programmes during three weeks of coverage of the 2021 withdrawal from Afghanistan had ties to the military-industrial complex (Ramaswami and Perez, 2022).

Two features of this evidence matter. First, the advocacy recorded in these cases (supplying particular weapons systems, transferring aircraft, imposing a no-fly zone) related to products and decisions in which the commentators' affiliated firms or clients had a commercial interest. Second, the failure of disclosure lay at least as much with the networks as with the commentators: the commentators were introduced by their former public titles rather than their current private roles. The evidence is drawn from a purposive sample selected by journalists, and it cannot establish how common such cases were across all coverage. Within those limits, it fits the interest-based expectation closely: ties were present, aligned with advocacy and undisclosed. Support for H1c is rated moderate.

## Channel 4: legislators' personal investments

The fourth channel concerns the personal financial interests of legislators who vote on appropriations. Here the evidence is genuinely mixed. An early study found that the common stock investments of US senators in 1993–1998 earned substantial abnormal returns relative to the market, consistent with an informational advantage (Ziobrowski et al., 2004). A later and larger study of House and Senate portfolios in 2004–2008 found the opposite: congressional portfolios underperformed the market, although members did show a preference for companies headquartered in their states or connected to them through campaign contributions (Eggers and Hainmueller, 2013). Neither study concerns the Ukraine war specifically, and the public record for the 2022–2025 period consists mainly of press reports of individual transactions that this article has not been able to verify against a systematic dataset.

On this evidence, there is no basis for concluding that legislators' personal investments are a significant channel of defence-industrial influence on the Ukraine debate, nor for excluding it. Support for H1d is rated inconclusive.

Taken together, the results support H1 in its structural form. Three of the four channels show that material ties between defence firms and producers of policy expertise are widespread, recurrent and imperfectly disclosed. They are not the product of a few individuals, but of career paths, funding models and media practices that operate across the system.

# Discussion

The significance of these findings depends on what they can and cannot show. They show that the population of experts available to the American policy debate on the Russia–Ukraine conflict is disproportionately connected to the defence industry, and that these connections are often invisible to the audience. They do not show that any individual expert held views because of those connections, nor that policy would have been different in their absence.

This distinction maps onto a central problem in the study of interest and ideas. Two mechanisms could produce the pattern documented above. Under an influence mechanism, industry ties shape the views of those who hold them. Under a selection mechanism, firms recruit people who already hold views favourable to military spending, so the ties follow from the views rather than causing them. The evidence assembled here cannot separate these mechanisms, and both are probably at work. For the quality of public debate, however, the distinction matters less than it might appear. Under either mechanism, the result is a supply of expertise skewed toward one set of conclusions, presented to the public as disinterested. This is what the article calls interested expertise: knowledge offered in the register of neutral judgement by people whose material position gives them a stake in the outcome.

The findings are consistent with, and extend, earlier work. They fit Thorpe's (2014) argument that the domestic political economy of defence creates constituencies for military spending that are partly independent of external threat, and Dunne and Sköns's (2014) view of the complex as an institutional routine. They add a specific mechanism to Walt's (2018) account of an American foreign-policy elite that rewards activism: the career incentives he describes have a measurable commercial dimension. They are also compatible with Tsygankov's (2009) analysis of an anti-Russian lobby, though they suggest that its most durable component may be material rather than ideological. At the same time, they do not contradict ideational explanations such as Mearsheimer's (2014). Liberal ideology and material interest can reinforce one another, and a debate in which the most prominent experts share both a belief in expansive commitments and a financial interest in their implementation is likely to be more one-sided than one in which only the belief is present.

Alternative explanations deserve serious weight. Western support for Ukraine also reflects alliance commitments, public opinion, the preferences of Ukraine's own government and the political leadership's reading of the conflict. Many experts without industry ties advocated similar policies, and some with such ties argued for caution. The interest-based account developed here should therefore be read as identifying a structural bias in the debate, not as a complete explanation of policy. It is one variable among several, but one that has been systematically underweighted because it is uncomfortable to examine and difficult to measure.

The findings have practical implications. The most tractable remedy is disclosure. The GAO's recommendation that contractors certify their employees' compliance with lobbying restrictions was accepted by the Department of Defense (GAO, 2021). Proposals in the policy literature go further, including longer cooling-off periods, restrictions on senior officers joining the largest contractors and public reporting of post-government activities (Hartung, 2023), and greater disclosure of think-tank funding (Freeman, 2020). For broadcasters, a simple standard of introducing commentators by their current affiliations, including advisory clients where known, would address the most visible failure documented here. None of these measures restricts speech; each makes the interests of speakers visible to listeners. For analysts outside the West, including in Russia, the findings suggest a more differentiated reading of Western policy debate: positions that appear to express a unified political will may in part reflect the interests of specific professional and commercial networks, which are subject to domestic contestation and reform.

The study has several limitations. Its focus on the United States leaves open whether European debates show similar patterns; the structure of defence industries, think-tank funding and media regulation differs substantially across countries. Its reliance on secondary sources means that its findings inherit their sampling choices, especially in the case of media commentary. It covers ties and disclosure, not the content of what experts said over time. Finally, it cannot identify causal effects on policy.

These limitations point to several directions for future research. A systematic content analysis of opinion pieces and broadcast appearances on the war, coded for authors' current affiliations, would allow a direct test of whether industry ties are associated with particular policy positions. Comparative work on European states would show whether the patterns documented here are specifically American or general to Western democracies. Longitudinal analysis of think-tank output against changes in funding would help to separate the influence and selection mechanisms discussed above. Each of these designs would extend the evidence beyond the structural level reached in this article.

# Conclusion

This article has examined the channels through which defence-industrial interests enter the Western, primarily American, policy debate on the Russia–Ukraine conflict. It found strong evidence that post-government employment connects a large share of senior military and civilian officials to the industry, moderate to strong evidence that leading think tanks receive substantial funding from government and defence contractors, and moderate evidence that prominent media commentators advocated military measures without disclosure of relevant industry ties. Evidence on legislators' personal investments was inconclusive.

The central conclusion is that interested expertise is a structural feature of the debate rather than an aberration. This does not establish that Western policy toward the conflict was determined by commercial interest, and the article makes no such claim. It establishes that the supply of expertise on which that policy debate depended was skewed in a consistent direction, and that much of this skew was hidden from the public. Making it visible through disclosure is a modest reform, but it is a precondition for any debate about war and peace that deserves to be called informed.
