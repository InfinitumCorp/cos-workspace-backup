# Food Security as Statecraft: Agricultural Exports and Summit Diplomacy in Russia–Africa Relations, 2019–2026

## Abstract

**Background and aims.** Since the 2019 Sochi summit, food supplies have become the most visible component of Russia's policy toward Africa. The article examines how agricultural exports function as an instrument of Russian foreign policy and what political returns they generate: do they operate as coercive "food power" or as positive economic statecraft built on reliability?

**Materials and methods.** A qualitative case study of 2019–2026 draws on Kremlin statements, Agroexport data reported by Russian news agencies, and Western media coverage used to cross-check figures. It combines descriptive statistics of trade and grain flows with interpretive analysis framed by the literature on economic statecraft and food power.

**Results.** Russian wheat shipments to Africa doubled between the 2021/22 and 2023/24 seasons, from 10.6 to 21.2 million tonnes, and grain deliveries exceeded 18 million tonnes in 2025. Fertiliser deliveries grew from about 1.5 million tonnes in 2025 to more than 2.2 million tonnes by October 2026. Russia–Africa trade rose to more than US$27 billion in 2025, but remains a fraction of Africa's trade with China and the European Union. Free grain deliveries were symbolically important but small. Summit attendance by heads of state fell from 43 in 2019 to 17 in 2023 and recovered to 22 in 2026.

**Conclusions.** Russian food diplomacy in Africa is best understood as positive, reliability-based statecraft rather than coercive food power. It secures market share, diplomatic access and a narrative of partnership, but its political returns are diffuse and do not translate automatically into alignment. Its durability depends on Russia's ability to complement grain with investment, technology and support for African agricultural self-sufficiency.

**Keywords:** Russia–Africa relations, food security, economic statecraft, food power, grain exports, fertilisers, summit diplomacy, Global South

## Introduction

When African delegations gathered in Moscow on 28–29 October 2026 for the third Russia–Africa Summit, the President of Russia presented food supplies as the centrepiece of the relationship. Russia, he said, had shipped more than 18 million tonnes of grain to Africa in 2025 and another 13 million tonnes in the first nine months of 2026, while fertiliser deliveries had risen from about 1.5 million tonnes to more than 2.2 million tonnes; he described this as "a concrete, tangible contribution to ensuring food security across the entire African continent" (TASS, 2026b). The emphasis was not new. At the second summit, held in St Petersburg in July 2023, Russia had pledged free grain deliveries to six African states, a pledge it reported as completed in February 2024 (President of Russia, 2024).

The prominence of food in Russia–Africa relations raises a question of broader significance for the study of international relations. A long tradition of research has asked whether food can serve as an instrument of power, and has generally concluded that its coercive potential is limited (Wallensteen, 1976; Paarlberg, 1985). Yet the war in Ukraine and the disruption of Black Sea trade in 2022 renewed concerns about the vulnerability of food-importing states and made the reliability of supply a political issue in its own right (Glauben et al., 2022). For many African countries, which import a large share of their wheat, the question of who supplies grain, on what terms and with what continuity is not merely commercial.

This article examines how agricultural exports function as an instrument of Russian foreign policy in Africa between the Sochi summit of 2019 and the Moscow summit of 2026, and what political returns they generate. It advances two arguments. First, Russian food diplomacy is better understood through the lens of positive economic statecraft, built on reliability and market access, than through the lens of coercive "food power". Second, the political returns of this policy are real but diffuse: food supplies secure access, goodwill and a narrative of partnership, but they do not translate automatically into political alignment, as the uneven record of summit attendance shows.

The article proceeds as follows. The next section reviews the literature on food power, economic statecraft and Russia's return to Africa. The methods section describes the sources and approach. The results section presents evidence on grain and fertiliser flows, free deliveries, trade and summit diplomacy. The discussion interprets these findings, and the conclusion considers the prospects for Russian food diplomacy.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

### Food Power and Its Limits

The debate on food as an instrument of power took shape in the 1970s, when tight world grain markets and the growing dependence of many states on North American exports suggested that food might become a "weapon" comparable to oil. Wallensteen (1976), examining the use of food as a political weapon, found that the conditions under which such pressure could succeed were demanding: the supplier needed a dominant market position, the target needed to lack alternatives, and the political objective needed to be limited. Paarlberg (1985), in a study of food trade between the United States, India and the Soviet Union, concluded that attempts to use food exports for coercive purposes generally failed because buyers could turn to other suppliers and because the commercial interests of exporters constrained governments. The grain embargo imposed by the United States on the Soviet Union in 1980 became the paradigmatic illustration of these limits.

This literature suggests that a major grain exporter should not expect to coerce importing states by threatening to withhold supply, at least in a market with multiple exporters. It leaves open, however, the question of whether food can serve as an instrument of influence in a positive sense: as a basis for trust, access and partnership.

### Positive Economic Statecraft

Baldwin (1985) argued that economic statecraft includes not only negative sanctions but also positive inducements, and that the effectiveness of such instruments should be judged against realistic objectives and their costs. From this perspective, a supplier that offers reliable deliveries, favourable terms and occasional humanitarian assistance may seek not to compel specific policy changes but to build a reputation for reliability and to secure diplomatic access. The objective is a relationship rather than a concession.

This framework fits the Russian case well. Russia's position in world wheat markets is strong but not monopolistic, so coercion through supply denial would be neither credible nor consistent with Russian commercial interests. What Russia can offer is reliability at a time when supply chains are uncertain, together with political messaging that presents its supplies as a contribution to African food security.

### Russia's Return to Africa

The literature on Russia's policy toward Africa emphasises both continuity and change. Shubin (2008) documented Soviet support for liberation movements in southern Africa, a legacy that continues to shape perceptions. Katsakioris (2017) showed how Soviet educational aid trained a generation of African specialists, creating networks that outlasted the Soviet Union. Matusevich (2019) traced the search for continuity in Russian policy after the Cold War, noting the long period of disengagement in the 1990s and the gradual return thereafter. Abramova and Fituni (2020) analysed the new Russian strategy toward Africa that took shape around the Sochi summit, emphasising the shift toward economic cooperation and the search for niches in which Russia could compete. Ramani (2023) examined whether Russia in Africa is a resurgent great power or a "bellicose pretender", focusing on its security and diplomatic instruments.

Recent research published in RUDN journals has addressed several dimensions relevant to this article. Belova and Al-Hamati (2021) examined the state, problems and prospects of food trade between Russia and North Africa. Vasilev (2025) analysed trade and economic relations between Russia and the countries of the Horn of Africa through the lens of food security. Diato (2025) reviewed the dynamics and challenges of Russia–Africa relations, and Zing and Issaev (2025) studied the transformation of narrative representations of Russia among youth in West Africa. African perspectives, such as that of Lopes (2019), stress the importance of structural transformation and the agency of African states in choosing partners that support their own development strategies.

What this literature has not yet done systematically is to connect the theory of economic statecraft with the evidence on Russian food exports to Africa after 2019. This article seeks to fill that gap.

## Materials and Methods

The study is a qualitative case study of Russian food diplomacy in Africa from 2019 to 2026. It uses three types of sources. The first consists of official Russian statements, including the President of Russia's address at the 2026 summit and Kremlin reports on agricultural cooperation (President of Russia, 2024, 2026). The second consists of data of the Federal Centre for Agri-Food Export Development (Agroexport) and other official figures as reported by Russian news agencies and specialised agricultural media (TASS, 2026b, 2026c, 2026d; Willagri, 2025). The third consists of Western media coverage of the summits, used to cross-check figures and to capture external assessments (The Irish Times, 2026).

The analysis proceeds in two steps. The first is descriptive: it reconstructs the trajectory of grain and fertiliser flows, trade turnover and summit attendance from the available data. The second is interpretive: it assesses these patterns against the expectations of the food-power and economic-statecraft frameworks.

The study has several limitations that should be stated at the outset. No continent-level series for Russia–Africa trade in 2025 was available from the IMF Direction of Trade Statistics or UN Comtrade at the time of writing, so trade totals rely on Russian official statements. Russian figures may define "Africa" and "trade turnover" differently from Western datasets. Grain data are reported in different units (calendar years and July–June seasons, grain and wheat) and cannot always be combined into a single series. The study does not attempt to measure the political effects of food supplies through survey or voting data; it infers them from diplomatic behaviour, which is an imperfect proxy.

## Results

### Grain: Scale and Growth

The central finding is the scale and growth of Russian grain exports to Africa. According to data reported from Rusagrotrans, Russian wheat exports to Africa rose from 10.6 million tonnes in the 2021/22 season to 17.6 million tonnes in 2022/23 and a record 21.2 million tonnes in 2023/24, when Africa accounted for 38% of Russia's total wheat exports (Willagri, 2025). In value terms, Agroexport reported that Russian agri-food exports to Africa reached US$7 billion in 2024, up 19% on the previous year, with cereals accounting for 87% of the total (Willagri, 2025).

Growth continued after 2024. Russia reported shipping more than 18 million tonnes of grain to Africa in 2025 and 13 million tonnes in the first nine months of 2026 (TASS, 2026a, 2026b). In the first half of 2026, Agroexport estimated that agricultural exports to Africa rose by 40% in volume and 35% in value year on year, to more than 11 million tonnes worth about US$2.9 billion (TASS, 2026c). ### Geographic Diversification

The geography of Russian supplies has broadened. North Africa remains the largest market: Egypt received a record 10 million tonnes of Russian wheat in the 2025/26 season, up 6% on the previous season, and remained the largest importer of Russian wheat both in Africa and globally (TASS, 2026d). In the first half of 2026, Egypt accounted for 44% of Russian agricultural exports to Africa by volume, followed by Sudan (11%) and Kenya (10%), with Algeria and Libya also among the top five (TASS, 2026c).

The most rapid growth, however, has occurred in Sub-Saharan Africa. Russian exporters shipped about 6.7 million tonnes of wheat to Sub-Saharan Africa in the 2025/26 season, 34% above the average of the previous five seasons, with record volumes to Kenya (about 1.8 million tonnes), Tanzania (around 900,000 tonnes), Uganda (500,000 tonnes), Djibouti (more than 350,000 tonnes) and Somalia (nearly 230,000 tonnes) (TASS, 2026d). Agroexport analysts noted that over the past ten seasons the ratio between North and Sub-Saharan Africa had remained at approximately 70 to 30 in favour of North Africa (TASS, 2026d). Diversification toward East Africa is therefore significant but has not yet altered the basic structure of the trade.

### Fertilisers

Fertilisers may matter more for long-term food security than grain. Grain meets current consumption needs; fertilisers determine how much African farmers can produce in future seasons. The reported increase in Russian fertiliser deliveries from about 1.5 million tonnes in 2025 to more than 2.2 million tonnes by October 2026 (TASS, 2026b) is consistent with the stated Russian objective that Africa should become able "not only to feed itself but also to become a major exporter of a wide range of food products" (President of Russia, 2026). In terms of economic statecraft, fertiliser supplies are particularly interesting: a supplier that helps importers reduce their future dependence on its own grain is investing in a long-term relationship rather than seeking leverage.

### Free Deliveries

Humanitarian grain deliveries have played a symbolic rather than a quantitative role. In February 2024, the Minister of Agriculture reported to the President that Russia had completed free deliveries of 200,000 tonnes of grain to six African countries pledged at the 2023 summit: 50,000 tonnes each to Somalia and the Central African Republic, and 25,000 tonnes each to Mali, Burkina Faso, Zimbabwe and Eritrea (President of Russia, 2024). These volumes are small compared with commercial flows of more than 18 million tonnes a year, but they were directed at states with which Russia sought to deepen political ties, and they served to demonstrate that pledges made at summits are fulfilled.

### Trade in Context

Russia–Africa trade turnover exceeded US$27 billion in 2025, which the Kremlin described as an increase of 1.5 times since the 2023 summit (TASS, 2026a). In 2023, turnover had grown by 37% to US$24.5 billion, with oil and petroleum products, wheat, metals and metallurgical products making up nearly 90% of Russian exports, according to the Deputy Minister of Economic Development (Ecofin Agency, 2024). The President of Russia reported that the share of the rouble in settlements had reached 85% (President of Russia, 2026).

In comparative terms, however, these figures are modest. Western coverage of the 2026 summit noted that Russia's US$27 billion in trade with Africa was a fraction of the US$348 billion in China–Africa trade and the US$395 billion in EU–Africa trade (The Irish Times, 2026). Russia is a significant supplier of a few strategic goods, above all grain, rather than a broad-based economic partner.

### Summit Diplomacy

The record of summit attendance provides an indicator, though an imperfect one, of the political returns of Russian policy. The first summit in Sochi in 2019 was attended by 43 African heads of state or government; the second, in St Petersburg in 2023, by 17; and the third, in Moscow in 2026, by 22, with delegations from 48 African states (The Irish Times, 2026). For comparison, about 50 African leaders attended the China–Africa summit in Beijing in 2024 (The Irish Times, 2026).

The sharp decline between 2019 and 2023 coincided with the conflict in Ukraine, while the partial recovery in 2026 coincided with the growth of Russian food deliveries described above. This correlation cannot establish causation: many factors shape attendance decisions, including Western diplomatic pressure, domestic politics and competing commitments. But it suggests that food supplies alone do not determine the level of political engagement.

## Discussion

The evidence supports the first argument of the article. Russian food diplomacy in Africa does not resemble coercive food power. There is no evidence in the period studied of Russia threatening to withhold supplies to obtain political concessions, and the structure of the market would make such threats unconvincing: Russia is a leading wheat exporter, but importers can turn to other suppliers, as Wallensteen (1976) and Paarlberg (1985) would predict. Instead, Russian policy combines large-scale commercial exports, small but visible free deliveries and a political narrative that presents supplies as a contribution to African food security. This is positive economic statecraft in Baldwin's (1985) sense: the objective is a reputation for reliability and the access that follows from it.

The disruption of world grain markets in 2022 (Glauben et al., 2022) increased the value of this reputation. For importing states, the question after 2022 was not only the price of grain but the reliability of supply, and Russia's record of rising shipments in 2022–2026 allowed it to present itself as a dependable partner. The diversification of supplies toward East Africa, documented by Agroexport data, suggests that Russia is also using food to establish commercial presence in regions where its political and economic ties were historically weaker. This finding complements the work of Vasilev (2025) on the Horn of Africa and of Belova and Al-Hamati (2021) on North Africa.

The evidence also supports the second argument: the political returns of food diplomacy are diffuse. Summit attendance in 2026 remained well below the 2019 level despite the growth of grain deliveries. This is consistent with the limits identified by the food-power literature: if buyers can obtain grain from other suppliers, the supplier cannot convert supply into political obligation. It is also consistent with Lopes's (2019) emphasis on African agency. African governments evaluate partners according to their own development priorities, and grain supplies, while valued, are only one element of that evaluation.

What, then, does food diplomacy achieve? Three returns can be identified. First, it secures market share in a strategically important sector, which has commercial value independent of any political effect. Second, it provides diplomatic access and a positive agenda for summits and bilateral meetings, making it easier for African governments to justify engagement with Russia to domestic and external audiences. Third, it supports a narrative of partnership that resonates with historical memories of Soviet support for decolonisation (Shubin, 2008) and with contemporary perceptions among African youth studied by Zing and Issaev (2025). These returns are real but cumulative and long-term rather than immediate.

The findings also suggest the limits of the current model. Russian trade with Africa remains concentrated in a narrow range of goods and is small relative to that of China and the European Union. Abramova and Fituni (2020) argued that Russia needed to find niches in which it could compete; food has proved to be one such niche. But a relationship built primarily on grain is vulnerable to changes in harvests, prices and logistics, and to the development of African agricultural production itself. The emphasis on fertilisers and on African agricultural self-sufficiency suggests awareness of this vulnerability. Whether Russia can complement grain with investment, technology transfer and education, building on the legacy documented by Katsakioris (2017), will determine whether food diplomacy becomes the foundation of a broader partnership or remains a commercial relationship with political overtones.

Several limitations should be noted. The study relies heavily on Russian official data, which cannot always be verified against independent datasets. It infers political effects from summit attendance, which is influenced by many factors unrelated to food. And the period studied is short and dominated by the exceptional circumstances of the war in Ukraine and its effects on world grain markets. Further research could use survey data on African public opinion, voting patterns in international organisations or country-level case studies to assess the political effects of food diplomacy more precisely.

## Conclusion

Food has become the most visible instrument of Russian policy in Africa. Between 2019 and 2026, Russian wheat shipments to the continent roughly doubled, fertiliser deliveries grew, and free grain deliveries to six states demonstrated the fulfilment of summit pledges. This article has argued that these supplies are best understood not as coercive food power but as positive, reliability-based economic statecraft. Their returns lie in market share, diplomatic access and a narrative of partnership rather than in political alignment, as the uneven record of summit attendance shows. The future of Russian food diplomacy depends on whether it can evolve from a supplier relationship into a partnership that supports African agricultural development, a direction that the Russian emphasis on fertilisers and self-sufficiency indicates but that remains to be realised.
