# Государственно направляемая адаптация: устойчивость российской экономики в условиях санкционного режима 2022–2025 гг.

**У.А. Тёрнер**

Infinitum Corporation (Вашингтон, США)

**Аннотация.** Санкционный режим, введённый против России после февраля 2022 г., стал крупнейшим в истории применения экономических санкций против крупной экономики. Международные финансовые институты и большинство западных аналитиков весной 2022 г. прогнозировали глубокий и затяжной спад. Этого не произошло: по данным МВФ, после сокращения на 1,4% в 2022 г. российская экономика выросла на 4,1% в 2023 г. и на 4,9% в 2024 г., а в 2025 г. рост замедлился до 1,0%. Цель статьи – объяснить расхождение между ожиданиями и результатом и оценить, что этот случай означает для теории экономических санкций. Исследовательский вопрос сформулирован так: какие институциональные особенности российской политико-экономической модели определили её способность адаптироваться к санкционному давлению? Гипотеза автора состоит в том, что устойчивость объясняется не только конъюнктурой сырьевых рынков, но прежде всего институтами государственно направляемого капитализма, сложившимися после кризиса 1998 г. и санкций 2014 г.: бюджетной дисциплиной и низким государственным долгом, суверенитетом денежно-кредитной политики, контролем государства над стратегическими отраслями и переориентацией внешней торговли и расчётов. Методологически работа основана на сравнении прогнозов с фактическими результатами, отслеживании процессов и анализе данных МВФ, Банка России, Минфина России и СИПРИ. Результаты подтверждают гипотезу по всем четырём механизмам, но показывают и цену адаптации: высокую инфляцию, ключевую ставку на уровне 21% в 2024–2025 гг., дефицит рабочей силы, рост военной нагрузки и снижение нефтегазовых доходов. Сделан вывод, что санкции против крупной экономики с низким долгом, гибким курсом, сильным центральным банком и альтернативными рынками сбыта способны повысить издержки, но не обрушить экономику. Это подкрепляет реалистскую критику санкций как инструмента принуждения и указывает на необходимость различать цели принуждения, сдерживания и сигнализирования. Выводы значимы для оценки дальнейшей эффективности санкций и для понимания роли государства в обеспечении экономической безопасности.

# State-Guided Adaptation: The Resilience of the Russian Economy under the 2022–2025 Sanctions Regime

**William A. Turner**

Infinitum Corporation (Washington, D.C., USA)

**Abstract.** The sanctions regime imposed on Russia after February 2022 is the largest ever applied to a major economy. In spring 2022, international financial institutions and most Western analysts forecast a deep and prolonged recession. It did not occur: according to the IMF, after contracting by 1.4% in 2022 the Russian economy grew by 4.1% in 2023 and 4.9% in 2024, before slowing to 1.0% in 2025. The purpose of this article is to explain the gap between expectation and outcome and to assess what the case implies for the theory of economic sanctions. The research question is: which institutional features of Russia's political economy determined its capacity to adapt to sanctions pressure? The author hypothesises that resilience is explained not only by commodity markets but above all by the institutions of state-guided capitalism built after the 1998 crisis and the 2014 sanctions: fiscal discipline and low public debt, monetary-policy sovereignty, state control of strategic sectors, and the reorientation of foreign trade and payments. The study combines a comparison of forecasts with outcomes, process tracing and analysis of data from the IMF, the Bank of Russia, the Ministry of Finance and SIPRI. The results support the hypothesis for all four mechanisms but also reveal the costs of adaptation: high inflation, a key rate of 21% in 2024–2025, labour shortages, a heavy military burden and falling oil and gas revenue. The article concludes that sanctions against a large economy with low debt, a flexible exchange rate, a strong central bank and alternative markets can raise costs but cannot bring about collapse. This supports the realist critique of sanctions as a coercive instrument and underlines the need to distinguish between the goals of coercion, constraint and signalling. The findings matter for assessing future sanctions and the role of the state in economic security.

**Keywords:** economic sanctions; sanctions effectiveness; state capitalism; Russian political economy; economic security; fiscal policy; monetary sovereignty; trade reorientation; national-currency settlements; weaponized interdependence

**For citation:** Turner W.A. State-Guided Adaptation: The Resilience of the Russian Economy under the 2022–2025 Sanctions Regime. *MGIMO Review of International Relations*. [issue details to be added by the editors]

# Introduction

In the weeks after February 2022, the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom, Japan and their partners imposed on Russia a sanctions regime without precedent in scope. It combined the immobilisation of a large share of the central bank's foreign reserves, the disconnection of major banks from international payment messaging, export controls on technology, restrictions on energy trade and thousands of designations of individuals, companies and vessels. By one count, the number of new list-based designations against Russia from the start of the conflict to August 2025 reached 23,960, more than twice the combined total for Iran, Syria and North Korea[^castellum]. The expectations attached to these measures were correspondingly high. In April 2022 the International Monetary Fund projected that the Russian economy would contract by 8.5% in 2022 and by a further 2.3% in 2023[^imf2022].

The outcome was different. On the IMF's current data, real GDP fell by 1.4% in 2022, then grew by 4.1% in 2023 and 4.9% in 2024, before slowing to 1.0% in 2025[^weo]. Unemployment fell every year, public debt remained below 17% of GDP and the current account stayed in surplus. The economy did not collapse; nor, as the slowdown of 2025 shows, did it escape significant costs. The purpose of this article is to explain why the forecasts were wrong and what this implies for the study of economic sanctions.

The research question is: which institutional features of Russia's political economy determined its capacity to adapt to sanctions pressure in 2022–2025? Existing answers fall into three groups. The first attributes Russian resilience mainly to commodity prices and the inelasticity of world demand for Russian energy and grain. The second emphasises the failure of enforcement, including the role of third countries in sustaining trade with Russia, an explanation that draws on research on "sanctions busting" (Early 2015). The third, which this article develops, holds that resilience was in large part the product of institutions built deliberately over two decades: what the article calls state-guided adaptation.

The hypothesis is that four institutional mechanisms, each traceable to policy choices made after the 1998 default and the sanctions of 2014, account for the gap between forecast and outcome: (1) fiscal discipline and low public debt; (2) monetary and financial sovereignty, including an autonomous central bank, a flexible exchange rate and a reserve policy designed to reduce exposure to Western custodians; (3) state control or heavy influence over strategic sectors, which allowed rapid reallocation of resources; and (4) the reorientation of trade and payments toward non-Western partners and national currencies. Commodity markets and third-country trade are treated not as rival explanations but as conditions whose effect depended on these institutions.

The significance of the question is twofold. For theory, the Russian case is the most important test of economic coercion against a large economy since the interwar period, and its results bear directly on long-running debates about whether sanctions work (Pape 1997; Hufbauer et al. 2007; Drezner 1999). For practice, governments on both sides continue to make decisions on the assumption that sanctions will, or will not, change outcomes over a given horizon. The article is organised as follows. After a review of the literature, the methodological section sets out the design and sources. The results section examines the four mechanisms and the costs of adaptation. The discussion compares the findings with other research and states the limitations of the study.

# Literature review

The academic debate on sanctions effectiveness is old and unresolved. Pape (1997) argued that economic sanctions rarely achieve major foreign-policy goals and that earlier optimistic findings overstated their success by miscoding cases in which force or other factors were decisive. Hufbauer, Schott, Elliott and Oegg (2007), working with the largest case database of its time, reported a more mixed record, with partial success in a substantial minority of cases. Drezner (1999) identified a "sanctions paradox": sanctions are most often imposed on adversaries, yet adversaries are the targets least likely to concede, because they expect continued conflict and weigh relative gains. Kirshner (1997) shifted attention to the domestic distributional effects of sanctions, arguing that their impact depends on which groups in the target state bear the costs and how the target government can redistribute them.

Russian scholarship has engaged extensively with this literature. Silaeva (2021) shows that conflicting conclusions about effectiveness stem largely from different conceptualisations of "sanctions" and "effectiveness", with a basic dividing line between realist and liberal understandings of sanctions as a foreign-policy tool. Rebro (2020), applying Giumelli's framework, distinguishes signalling, constraining and coercing functions of US sanctions against Russia and argues that the regime created in 2014 is inherently inert and likely to persist regardless of shifts in the priorities of US leadership. Giumelli (2011) himself established this tripartite distinction as a way of evaluating sanctions against their actual purposes rather than a single standard of compliance. Timofeev (2019) proposed rethinking sanctions efficiency in a similar spirit. Borovsky (2019) traced Western restrictive measures against the Soviet and Russian energy sector from the late 1940s onward and posed the question of whether they reflect political rivalry or economic competition. After 2022, Lebedev (2022) argued that the real purpose of the new sanctions was not to change Russian foreign policy but to weaken the country economically, so that they should be analysed as economic warfare, and that a target cannot resist large-scale sanctions in the long term with purely defensive measures.

A second body of literature concerns the transformation of sanctions in an era of global networks. Farrell and Newman (2019) argue that states controlling central nodes of global financial and information networks can "weaponize interdependence" by surveilling flows and denying access. They also note that such use creates incentives for targets to build alternative networks. Mulder (2022) shows that the "economic weapon" was conceived in the interwar period as an instrument of war rather than peace, which resonates with Lebedev's interpretation. Demarais (2022) argues that the extensive use of US sanctions is reshaping the world economy in ways that work against US interests, including by encouraging alternatives to the dollar. Drezner (2011) examines the shift to "smart" or targeted sanctions and their limited coercive effect. Datasets such as the Threat and Imposition of Economic Sanctions database (Morgan, Bapat, Kobayashi 2014) and the Global Sanctions Data Base (Felbermayr et al. 2020) document the rising frequency of sanctions, which in itself makes adaptation by targets more likely.

A third body of literature concerns the Russian economic system itself. Gaddy and Ickes (2013) describe a system organised around the distribution of resource rents, which they regard as an obstacle to modernisation. Yakovlev (2006) traced the shift in business–state relations from "state capture" in the 1990s toward a model in which the state disciplined large business. Miller (2018) characterises the economic policy of the 2000s and 2010s as a combination of macroeconomic conservatism, support for strategic firms and management of social stability, a combination he regards as more coherent than critics allow. Åslund (2019), by contrast, characterises the system as crony capitalism that sacrifices growth to the interests of a narrow elite. In comparative political economy, Musacchio and Lazzarini (2014) distinguish among forms of state capitalism, from the state as majority owner to the state as minority investor or lender, a typology that helps to situate the Russian case. Wigell and Vihma (2016) analyse the interplay of geopolitical and geoeconomic strategies in Russian relations with the European Union.

Closest to the present article is Connolly (2018), who showed that the sanctions of 2014 did not weaken but strengthened the role of the state in the Russian economy, accelerating import substitution and the reorientation of trade and finance. Gurvich and Prilepskiy (2015) estimated the macroeconomic cost of the 2014 financial sanctions and found it substantial, though smaller than that of the simultaneous fall in oil prices. Ershov (2022) described 2022 as the culmination of a thirty-year "test of strength" for the Russian economy and identified the need to rebuild the mechanisms of economic relations under new constraints.

This literature leaves a gap. Studies of sanctions effectiveness tend to treat the target's economy as a passive object, while studies of the Russian economy tend to treat sanctions as one shock among many. The present article joins the two by asking how specific institutions of the target state mediated the effects of a specific sanctions regime.

# Analytical framework

The article builds its framework from two insights in the literature. The first, from Kirshner (1997), is that the effect of sanctions on a target depends less on the aggregate loss of trade than on how the target state distributes and absorbs the costs. A sanction that removes 1% of GDP can be politically devastating if it falls on a group whose support the government needs, and nearly irrelevant if the state can spread it thinly or shift it over time. The second, from Farrell and Newman (2019), is that the power of the sender rests on its control of network chokepoints, and that this power decays as targets build alternatives. Together they suggest that a target's resilience depends on two capacities: the capacity to absorb and redistribute costs internally, and the capacity to route around the sender's chokepoints externally.

The four mechanisms examined below map onto these capacities. Fiscal discipline and low debt (Mechanism 1) and state control of strategic sectors (Mechanism 3) are capacities for internal absorption and redistribution: they determine whether the state can finance higher spending, compensate affected groups and redirect production without a financial crisis. Monetary and financial sovereignty (Mechanism 2) and the reorientation of trade and payments (Mechanism 4) are capacities for routing around chokepoints: they determine whether the economy can continue to earn, hold and use foreign exchange when Western financial infrastructure is closed to it.

This framing has a historical dimension that is important for the Russian case. The sanctions of 2014, imposed in response to the events in Crimea and Donbass, restricted access to Western capital markets for major state banks and energy companies and limited technology transfers to the oil sector. They coincided with a sharp fall in oil prices, and Gurvich and Prilepskiy (2015) found that the oil shock had the larger macroeconomic effect, while the financial sanctions nonetheless imposed a substantial cost through reduced access to external borrowing. Connolly (2018) showed that the policy response to 2014, including import substitution, the development of domestic financial infrastructure and the strengthening of state-directed investment, made the state more central to the economy. In terms of the present framework, the period 2014–2021 was one in which both capacities were deliberately strengthened. The question for 2022–2025 is whether those investments paid off under a far more severe regime.

The framework also generates expectations that could have disconfirmed the hypothesis. If resilience were explained mainly by commodity prices, the economy should have weakened sharply once energy prices and oil and gas revenue fell. If it were explained mainly by weak enforcement, the institutional preconditions identified here should be absent or irrelevant, and adaptation should have depended on ad hoc trade diversion rather than on pre-existing state capacity. The results section returns to these expectations.

# Research design and methods

The study is a single-case study using three complementary methods.

The first is a comparison of forecasts with outcomes. The forecasts made in spring 2022 by international financial institutions represent the best available statement of expert expectations under the assumption that sanctions would have a severe effect. Their divergence from later data indicates which assumptions failed.

The second is process tracing. For each of the four hypothesised mechanisms, the study identifies the institutional precondition (the policy choice that created it, and when), the mechanism's operation in 2022–2025, and observable implications. The hypothesis is supported for a mechanism if the precondition existed before 2022 and the observable implications appear in the data.

The third is descriptive analysis of macroeconomic indicators for 2021–2025. The principal sources are the IMF World Economic Outlook database (October 2026 vintage), the Bank of Russia, the Russian Ministry of Finance, Rosstat as reported by news agencies, SIPRI and Chinese customs data. All statistical and documentary sources are given in footnotes, as the journal requires; the list of references contains only scholarly literature.

The study does not attempt a counterfactual estimate of how the Russian economy would have performed without sanctions, which would require a structural model beyond its scope. Nor does it attempt to separate precisely the effects of sanctions from those of the wartime fiscal stimulus. These limitations are discussed below.

The pattern is a shock, a rebound and a slowdown. It is not the pattern of a sanctions-induced financial crisis, which would show rising unemployment, a balance-of-payments crisis and a jump in public debt. Each of the four mechanisms helps to explain why.

## Mechanism 1: fiscal discipline and low public debt

*Precondition.* The default of August 1998, when the government restructured its domestic debt and imposed a moratorium on private external payments, left a durable lesson in Russian policy-making: dependence on external creditors is a source of vulnerability. In the following two decades the authorities kept public debt low, accumulated a sovereign wealth fund and, from the late 2010s, operated a fiscal rule that saved a share of oil and gas revenue. Miller (2018) identifies macroeconomic conservatism of this kind as a central pillar of economic policy in the period.

*Operation.* General government gross debt stood at 16.5% of GDP in 2021 and remained between 14.8% and 16.6% throughout 2022–2025 (Table 2). In comparison, the corresponding figures for 2025 were 62.7% in Germany and 124.4% in the United States[^debt]. Because Russia's public debt was low and largely domestic, the closure of Western capital markets removed a source of financing that the state did not need. The federal budget deficit in 2025 was 2.6% of GDP, or 5.6 trillion roubles, on the Ministry of Finance's preliminary estimate[^minfin]. The government financed rising expenditure through domestic borrowing, the National Wealth Fund and tax measures, including a progressive personal income tax scale from 2025[^tax].

*Observable implications.* If this mechanism operated, the state should have been able to increase spending sharply without a debt crisis. That is what occurred. SIPRI estimates Russian military expenditure in 2025 at $190 billion, or 7.5% of GDP and 20% of government spending[^sipri]. Despite this, the debt ratio in 2025 was almost the same as in 2021. The composition of fiscal measures is also informative. Faced with rising expenditure, the authorities combined higher taxation, including the progressive personal income tax scale from 2025 and the Ministry of Finance's proposal of September 2025 to raise value-added tax from 20% to 22%, with drawdowns of the National Wealth Fund and domestic borrowing[^tax][^vat]. The reliance on taxes and accumulated savings rather than on monetary financing or external borrowing is consistent with the macroeconomic conservatism that Miller (2018) describes. The IMF projects that Russia's debt ratio will rise to 19.3% of GDP in 2026 and 22.2% in 2027, a deterioration from a very low base[^debt]. The mechanism is supported.

## Mechanism 2: monetary and financial sovereignty

*Precondition.* After 2014 the Bank of Russia moved to a floating exchange rate and inflation targeting, and the authorities developed a domestic payment-messaging system and a national card system as insurance against disconnection. The central bank also increased the share of gold in its reserves.

*Operation.* The immobilisation of a large part of the central bank's reserves held abroad was the most dramatic measure of 2022 and a real loss. Its macroeconomic effect was limited, however, by three factors. First, capital controls and the requirement for exporters to sell foreign-currency revenue stabilised the exchange rate in the acute phase, a policy combination whose logic has been analysed in recent work on sanctions and the exchange rate[^itskhoki]. Second, the current-account surplus, which reached 10.4% of GDP in 2022 (Table 2), supplied foreign currency through trade rather than reserves. Third, the central bank retained full autonomy over interest-rate policy. It used that autonomy aggressively, raising the key rate to a record 21% in October 2024, holding it until June 2025 and then lowering it gradually to 14% by October 2026[^cbr]. The share of gold in Russia's international reserves stood at 41.2% at the end of September 2026[^gold], a composition that reflects the lesson of 2022: reserves held with Western custodians can be frozen.

*Observable implications.* If the mechanism operated, the 2022 shock should have produced inflation and currency volatility but not a banking crisis or a sustained loss of monetary control. Inflation did rise to an average of 13.7% in 2022 and remained high, at 8.7% in 2025 (Table 2), but there was no systemic banking crisis, and the central bank was able to tighten policy decisively when the economy overheated. The mechanism is supported, with the important qualification that monetary sovereignty came at the price of very expensive credit.

## Mechanism 3: state control of strategic sectors

*Precondition.* By the time sanctions were imposed, the state already held a large share of the economy. An IMF working paper estimated the state's footprint at about 33% of GDP in 2016, comprising 13.5% for general government and 19.3% for state-owned enterprises[^imf2019]. Energy, the largest banks, transport infrastructure and the defence industry were under state control or heavy state influence. Yakovlev (2006) and Connolly (2018) describe how this structure emerged: first through the reassertion of state authority over large business in the 2000s, then through the response to the 2014 sanctions, which expanded state-directed lending and import substitution.

*Operation.* In 2022–2025 this structure allowed the state to redirect resources quickly. State banks extended credit to sanctioned sectors, state-owned enterprises absorbed losses that private owners might not have accepted, and the defence-industrial sector expanded production on the basis of state orders. In Musacchio and Lazzarini's (2014) typology, the Russian state acted simultaneously as majority owner, lender and customer. This form of state capitalism is often criticised for inefficiency, and Gaddy and Ickes (2013) and Åslund (2019) have documented its costs to long-term productivity. Under conditions of acute external pressure, however, its capacity for rapid reallocation proved an asset.

*Observable implications.* If the mechanism operated, output in state-directed sectors should have expanded, unemployment should have fallen and the composition of demand should have shifted toward the state. Unemployment fell from 4.8% in 2021 to 2.2% in 2025 (Table 2), and the scale of military expenditure recorded by SIPRI indicates a large shift in the composition of demand. The mechanism is supported. It also explains part of the subsequent overheating: a state that can reallocate resources quickly can also create labour shortages and inflationary pressure.

## Mechanism 4: reorientation of trade and payments

*Precondition.* Russia's turn toward Asian markets predated 2022, and after 2014 the authorities had promoted settlements in national currencies with major partners. Farrell and Newman (2019) predicted that the weaponisation of financial networks would encourage targets to build alternatives; Russia had begun to do so before 2022.

*Operation.* After 2022 trade shifted decisively toward China, India, Türkiye and other non-Western partners. China–Russia trade reached $228.1 billion in 2025, down 6.9% from 2024 but above $200 billion for the third consecutive year[^china]. In settlements, the Bank of Russia reported that the rouble accounted for 53.7% of export and 54.4% of import payments in Russian foreign trade in 2025, while the share of the dollar and euro fell below 15%[^settle]. Russian officials reported that 90–95% of trade with China and India was settled in national currencies[^tass]. Grain illustrates the limits of isolation in a different way: Russia exported about 46.5 million tonnes of wheat in the 2025/26 season, remaining the world's largest exporter[^wheat].

*Observable implications.* If the mechanism operated, Russia should have maintained a current-account surplus and trade volumes despite the loss of Western markets, while bearing costs in prices, logistics and discounts. The current account remained in surplus throughout 2022–2025 (Table 2). At the same time, empirical research on Russian oil exports finds that the sanctions, including the price cap, reduced export revenue by widening the discount on Russian crude[^babina]. The mechanism is supported, with costs.

## The costs of adaptation

The four mechanisms explain why the economy did not collapse. They do not imply that sanctions were costless. Several costs are visible in the data. Inflation remained well above the Bank of Russia's target throughout the period. The key rate of 21%, held for eight months, imposed heavy costs on borrowers and investment[^cbr]. Growth slowed from 4.9% in 2024 to 1.0% in 2025[^weo], and the Bank of Russia forecasts 0.5–1.0% for 2026[^cbr]. Unemployment of 2.2% signals a labour shortage rather than full employment in the conventional sense. Oil and gas revenue to the federal budget fell by 23.8% in 2025 to 8.477 trillion roubles, mainly because of lower oil prices[^oilgas]. Military expenditure of 7.5% of GDP[^sipri] diverts resources from civilian investment. Some of these costs result from sanctions, some from the wartime fiscal stimulus and some from deliberate monetary tightening; separating them precisely is beyond the scope of this study.

# Discussion

The results support the hypothesis. All four mechanisms had institutional preconditions established before 2022, and the observable implications of each appear in the data for 2022–2025. The most important mechanism was probably the first. A state with high or largely external debt could not have financed a large increase in expenditure while cut off from Western capital markets; Russia's low and largely domestic debt turned the closure of those markets from a crisis into an inconvenience. The second and fourth mechanisms worked together: the current-account surplus and the reorientation of trade supplied the foreign exchange that the frozen reserves could not. The third mechanism explains the speed of adaptation, but also part of its cost.

How do these findings relate to existing research? They are broadly consistent with the realist critique of sanctions as an instrument of coercion (Pape 1997) and with Drezner's (1999) argument that adversaries anticipating prolonged conflict are least likely to concede. They support the argument of Farrell and Newman (2019) that weaponising interdependence creates incentives for targets to exit Western networks, and the related concern expressed by Demarais (2022). They extend Connolly's (2018) analysis of the 2014 sanctions: the institutional responses he documented became the foundations of resilience after 2022. They also lend weight to Kirshner's (1997) emphasis on the domestic distribution of costs. The Russian state was able to shift costs across groups and over time, through the budget, the central bank and state-owned enterprises, in ways that blunted the political effect of sanctions.

The findings also bear on the debate among Russian scholars. They are consistent with Silaeva's (2021) argument that judgements of effectiveness depend on how goals are defined. If the goal was coercion, that is, a change in Russian policy, the sanctions failed. If the goal was constraint, that is, raising the costs of Russian action, they partially succeeded: the costs listed above are real. If the goal was signalling, they succeeded in the narrow sense of demonstrating Western resolve. In this respect the findings support Rebro's (2020) distinction between signalling, constraining and coercing, and Giumelli's (2011) insistence that sanctions be evaluated against their actual purposes. They are also compatible with Lebedev's (2022) interpretation of sanctions as economic warfare aimed at weakening rather than persuading. If that was the aim, the relevant question becomes whether Russia's "defensive" adaptation will prove sufficient in the long term, which Lebedev doubts. The slowdown of 2025 suggests the question remains open.

The findings have a further implication for the debate on "smart" sanctions. Drezner (2011) observed that the move to targeted sanctions after the 1990s was motivated by the humanitarian costs of comprehensive embargoes but had not markedly improved their coercive effect. The Russian regime of 2022 combined targeted designations on a scale never seen before with sectoral and financial measures close to comprehensive in their intent. The Russian experience suggests that scale does not overcome the limits Drezner identified when the target can absorb costs internally. Designations of individuals and companies impose real burdens on those targeted, but the state can reallocate assets, transfer ownership and compensate losses through the budget and state banks, a process that the third mechanism describes. In this sense, the very structure of state-guided capitalism that critics such as Åslund (2019) regard as a source of inefficiency also made targeted sanctions easier to absorb.

The geoeconomic perspective of Wigell and Vihma (2016) adds another layer. They emphasise that Russian strategy toward Europe combined geopolitical and geoeconomic instruments, with energy interdependence as a central lever. After 2022 that lever largely disappeared as European states reduced their imports of Russian energy, but its disappearance cut both ways: it removed Russian leverage over Europe and Western leverage over Russia derived from the same interdependence. The reorientation of trade toward Asia documented under the fourth mechanism is, from this perspective, a geoeconomic realignment rather than merely a technical adaptation, and its long-term consequences for the structure of the world economy, which Demarais (2022) anticipates, may outlast the conflict that triggered it.

There are alternative explanations that deserve consideration. The commodity-price explanation is the most common. High energy prices in 2022 undoubtedly helped, as the current-account surplus of that year shows. But commodity prices fell after 2022, and oil and gas revenue declined sharply in 2025, yet the economy did not collapse. Commodity income was a condition, not a sufficient cause; its effect depended on the institutions that saved, allocated and redirected it. The sanctions-busting explanation (Early 2015) also has merit: trade through third countries clearly mattered. But the willingness of third countries to trade with Russia was itself conditioned by the existence of payment channels in national currencies and by Russian state policy, which links this explanation to the fourth mechanism.

The study has several limitations. First, as a single-case study it cannot establish how far its conclusions generalise. The combination of size, resource wealth, low debt and alternative markets that characterises Russia is rare, and smaller or more indebted targets may be more vulnerable. Second, it relies on official data, including Russian official data, which some analysts treat with caution, and on IMF series that are themselves partly based on official statistics. Third, it does not estimate a counterfactual and cannot separate the effects of sanctions from those of the wartime fiscal stimulus. Fourth, the period studied is short in relation to the long-term effects of technology restrictions, which may take a decade to become fully visible.

These limitations suggest directions for further research. Comparative work on other large targets, such as Iran and, potentially, China, would test whether the four mechanisms identified here generalise. Sectoral studies of technology-intensive industries would show whether export controls impose long-term costs that macroeconomic data do not yet capture. Research on third-country trade networks would clarify the respective roles of Russian policy and partner-country incentives. Finally, the experience of 2022–2025 calls for a theoretical re-examination of sanctions against major powers, an area in which existing theory, developed largely on cases of small and dependent targets, appears least reliable.

# Conclusion

The sanctions imposed on Russia after February 2022 did not produce the economic collapse that was widely forecast. The article has argued that this outcome is explained above all by four institutional mechanisms of state-guided capitalism: fiscal discipline and low public debt, monetary and financial sovereignty, state control of strategic sectors, and the reorientation of trade and payments. These institutions were built deliberately in response to the crisis of 1998 and the sanctions of 2014, and they allowed the Russian state to absorb, redistribute and offset the costs of sanctions. Adaptation was not costless: high inflation, very high interest rates, labour shortages, a heavy military burden and slowing growth are the price. But the main conclusion stands. Sanctions against a large economy with low debt, a flexible exchange rate, a strong central bank and access to alternative markets can raise costs but cannot force collapse, and the theory of sanctions needs to take the institutions of the target state seriously.
